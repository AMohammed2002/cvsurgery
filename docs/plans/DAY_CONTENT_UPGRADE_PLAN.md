# CardiacSchool — Day-by-Day Content Upgrade Plan
## A five-sequence work order for taking each module from med-student entry to a *true* PGY-5 exit

**Prepared:** 2026-07-10
**Revision:** 2.0 (deep-research upgrade — expanded depth, grounded evidence vault, and an explicit authoring workflow for the content agent)
**Author intent:** This is a build specification handed to Claude (or any competent content agent). It describes *how* to rewrite the per-day teaching content of CardiacSchool so that each day genuinely begins at the level of a medical student who has never scrubbed a heart case and ends at the level a senior (PGY-5) cardiac surgery resident is held to. It is deliberately long and prescriptive because the last attempt failed for one specific reason, corrected in Part 0, and that failure must not repeat. This 2.0 revision keeps every structural decision of the 1.0 plan and adds four things the first version only gestured at: (1) the *actual verified reference figures* — trial numbers, doses, thresholds, and anatomic measurements — that the content must hit, collected in Appendix C so the author is never guessing; (2) an explicit, step-by-step **authoring workflow** the agent runs for every day (Part 5); (3) a **research protocol** that tells the agent exactly which sources to consult and how to handle any figure it cannot verify; and (4) deepened per-day specs in Part 3 that name the specific numbers, sutures, and trial critiques rather than pointing at them.

**Scope of this document:** the *content* — the lessons, the flashcards, the questions, the operative narratives, the viva prompts, the physiology labs, and the crises — not the app chrome. Do **not** re-add anything to the 14-Day Plan *page* (`curriculum.js` / `viewPlan()` / `style.css`); that experiment was reverted on purpose. The plan page will surface the upgraded content automatically once it exists in the per-day data files.

**How to read it:** Part 0 is the calibration correction — read it first and internalize it, because it is the reason the previous pass was rejected. Part 1 is the recalibrated five-level knowledge ladder with worked examples that make the gap between "interested medical student" and "PGY-5" visceral. Part 2 is the universal authoring contract every upgraded day must satisfy, now carrying the grounded quantitative floors. Part 3 is the five sequences — the actual ordered build plan, with each day's PGY-5 content specified in detail inside the sequence that owns it. Part 4 is execution, validation, and the acceptance bar. Part 5 — new in this revision — is the operational authoring workflow: the exact motions the agent goes through to produce one day of content, including how it researches, drafts, tags, validates, and self-audits against the calibration bar. The appendices carry the five worked ladders (A), the exemplar item register (B), the grounded evidence-and-numbers vault with sources (C), the per-day authoring checklist templates (D), and the source bibliography (E).

**A note on how the numbers in this document should be used.** Every specific figure in Parts 2–3 and Appendix C has been checked against a current source at the time of writing (July 2026) and is cited in Appendix E. They are given so the content agent has a *verified anchor* and does not fabricate. But medicine moves, and a build specification is not a clinical reference. The standing rule from Part 2.3 holds absolutely: the agent teaches the current guideline statement, and any figure it cannot itself re-verify against a primary or guideline source at authoring time ships with the qualitative claim intact and a `<!-- VERIFY: ... -->` marker, never as a confident bare assertion. Better an honest flag than a stale number stated with authority.

## PART 0 — THE CALIBRATION CORRECTION (read this before anything else)

The previous upgrade pass was rejected for a single, fatal reason: **the level bar was set roughly two rungs too low, and every rung was mislabeled.** What that pass labeled "Chief / PGY-5 exit" was, in reality, the knowledge of *a strong medical student who is interested in cardiac surgery and has read ahead.* It was not resident knowledge, let alone senior-resident knowledge.

Concretely, the rejected pass called the following a "PGY-5" competency for Day 1 (anatomy):

> "For every danger structure — circumflex under the P1 annulus, AV node in Koch's triangle, phrenic on the pericardium and LIMA, His at the membranous septum, RCA ostium in SAVR — name the maneuver that threatens it and the one that protects it."

That is a fine **medical-student-interested-in-the-field** objective. A PGY-5 does not merely *name* the maneuver that threatens the circumflex; they can tell you the exact segmental relationship of the artery to the P1/P2 annulus in millimeters and how it varies with left- versus right-dominance, they modify their annuloplasty suture depth and bite angle at the lateral annulus in real time to stay out of it, they recognize on the post-bypass TEE the new lateral wall-motion abnormality that says they caught it, they know the reoperative and endovascular options to rescue an injured circumflex, and they can teach the junior why the standard teaching ("stay superficial laterally") is necessary but not sufficient. The gap between "name the maneuver" and "own the decision and its rescue" is the whole difference between a student and a surgeon.

**The rule for this entire rebuild:** every time you are tempted to write a "PGY-5" objective, ask, *"Could a well-read fourth-year medical student say this sentence after one good textbook chapter?"* If the answer is yes, it is not a PGY-5 objective — it is a foundation objective, and the real PGY-5 objective sits two levels above it. Push until the objective requires **operative judgment, quantitative intraoperative reasoning, trial-level evidentiary command, or management of a rare lethal event with specific doses and thresholds** — things you only get from doing the work, not from reading about it.

### 0.1 The anatomy of the failure (so it is recognizable, not just named)

The rejected pass shared four recurring tells. Learn them, because they are how *this* pass will fail too if calibration drifts, and the depth validator in Sequence 1 is built to detect exactly these:

1. **The naked number.** A threshold stated without its conditions or exceptions — "severe AS is a mean gradient ≥ 40 mmHg," full stop. A senior resident never holds a threshold as a bare fact; they hold it as a *flow-dependent relationship* with the discordant cases and the resolving test attached. A day full of naked numbers is a flashcard deck wearing a lab coat.

2. **The name-drop trial.** A landmark study invoked by name and one-line conclusion — "FREEDOM showed CABG is better in diabetics" — with none of the design, the effect size, the tradeoff, or the criticism that lets a senior *use* the trial to defend a decision in front of someone arguing the other side. A name is recall; a defended trial is Level 5.

3. **The sewn-in valve.** An operation summarized rather than narrated — "the valve is excised and the prosthesis is sewn in." That is a Level-1 sentence. The senior narrates the decalcification without perforating the annulus, the suture technique and where it must avoid the conduction axis, the sizing decision and the mismatch it prevents, the de-airing ritual, and the three things that can go wrong at *that* step with their rescues.

4. **The complication as a noun.** Listing "heart block, paravalvular leak, coronary obstruction" as risks without the recognition pattern, the intraoperative decision that prevents each, and the dosed algorithm that rescues it when it happens anyway. A senior does not have a *list* of complications; they have a *response* to each.

If a day exhibits any of these four tells in its Level-4/5 layers, it is the rejected pass wearing new prose, and it must go back.

### 0.2 A worked before/after (the shape of a real fix)

To make the correction concrete, here is a single objective rewritten from the rejected level to the target level. Every day's mastery layer should read like the "after," not the "before."

- **Rejected ("PGY-5") version, Day 8:** *"Know that patient-prosthesis mismatch is a risk of aortic valve replacement and that a larger valve is better."*
- **Target Level-5 version, Day 8:** *"Predict patient-prosthesis mismatch before the incision from the indexed effective orifice area the chosen prosthesis will deliver for this patient's body surface area — moderate mismatch below roughly 0.85 cm²/m² and severe below roughly 0.65 cm²/m² — recognize that mismatch matters most in the small, active, or low-EF patient, and know the intraoperative decision tree when the annulus measures smaller than the valve you sized for: accept the mismatch, choose a supra-annular or a modern externally-mounted leaflet valve to buy an effective-orifice size, or perform an annular enlargement (Nicks/Manouguian posterior enlargement or the aortoventriculoplasty for the truly small root) — and defend which of those you would do in a 62-year-old active patient with a 19 mm annulus, against an examiner arguing you should just have placed the smaller valve and moved on."*

Notice what changed: the naked risk became a *quantified prediction* with the index and its thresholds; the "bigger is better" folk rule became a *named set of technical options* with the anatomy that selects among them; and the whole thing became a *defended decision under adversarial questioning.* That is the two-rung climb, made concrete.

**The rule restated for the rebuild:** every time you are tempted to write a "PGY-5" objective, ask, *"Could a well-read fourth-year medical student say this sentence after one good textbook chapter?"* If yes, it is a foundation objective, and the real PGY-5 objective sits two levels above it — in operative judgment, quantitative intraoperative reasoning, trial-design command, or the dosed management of a rare lethal event.

The rebuild's job is to make each day *actually climb that far.* That means the per-day content must contain the deep material, not gesture at it. A day is not "upgraded to PGY-5" because its summary paragraph claims a PGY-5 objective; it is upgraded when the lessons, cards, questions, and operative narratives on that day contain enough real, specific, quantitative, evidence-anchored, decision-forcing material that a learner who masters all of it could actually defend themselves in a senior-resident viva. Prose that promises depth it does not deliver is the exact failure mode to avoid.

## PART 1 — THE RECALIBRATED FIVE-LEVEL KNOWLEDGE LADDER

Every day of content must span this ladder. The five levels below are defined so precisely that any objective you write can be placed on exactly one rung. Author to the top of the ladder while keeping the bottom rung genuinely gentle.

### Level 1 — Medical student, novice (the honest entry point)
Has finished pre-clinical cardiology. Can name the four chambers, four valves, and the great vessels; knows systole from diastole; recognizes the words "stenosis," "regurgitation," "ischemia," "bypass," and "ejection fraction"; can read a normal versus grossly abnormal ECG; has never seen a sternotomy. Learns by narrative, analogy, and one clean diagram at a time. **This is where each day must start. Do not skip it — the on-ramp is what makes the climb credible.**

### Level 2 — Sub-intern / PGY-1 intern (floor and orders competence)
Can pre-round, collect and present post-op data, write the routine order set, manage chest tubes, pacing wires, central lines, and drains, remove them, dose the common drips at a starting rate, recognize the sick patient and escalate, and first-assist in the OR (retract, cut suture, follow the case, harvest a saphenous vein under supervision). Knows the vocabulary of the OR and the ICU and the normal post-op trajectory. Does **not** yet make operative decisions or run a crisis alone.

### Level 3 — Junior resident (PGY-2 to early PGY-3; conceptual operative fluency)
Understands the operative flow of the common cases and can perform defined portions (open and close, cannulate, come off bypass with the attending's hand nearby). Reasons through the physiology conceptually and quantitatively at the level of "why," knows the guideline indications and the headline trials by name and conclusion, runs a routine ICU, and recognizes the common complications early. Can explain a case to a student. Still needs the attending for the hard intraoperative call and the rare crisis.

### Level 4 — Senior resident (PGY-4 to PGY-5; near-independent judgment)
Runs the routine major cases as operating surgeon under supervision, makes the intraoperative decisions and states the reasons, modifies technique for the difficult anatomy, anticipates and manages the intraoperative complication before it declares, runs the unit including the deteriorating patient, and commands the evidence at the level of trial design, subgroup nuance, and its criticisms. Teaches juniors. This is the working definition of "senior resident competence."

### Level 5 — PGY-5 exit / chief / early fellow (the target ceiling of every day)
The near-graduate. Can narrate and reason through the operation as though about to do it as the primary surgeon: the exposure, the tissue planes, the specific sutures and bite depths, the sizing decisions, the bailouts, the conversions, and the intraoperative surprises and their rescues. Reasons in curves and equations, not sentences — derives the physiology rather than recalling it. Commands the evidence to the level of enrollment criteria, endpoint definitions, absolute versus relative effect sizes, the safety tradeoffs, the follow-up limitations, and the live controversies and how they moved the guidelines. Manages the rare lethal events with a specific, ordered, dosed algorithm. Knows when **not** to operate, when to abort, and when to convert. Can defend every decision three levels deep — mechanism, evidence, exception — under adversarial questioning, and can teach all of it.

### Worked calibration example A — "Complete heart block after aortic valve replacement"
This single problem, shown at all five levels, is the reference standard for how far each day must climb. Use it as your yardstick.

- **Level 1 (med student):** "The heart's electrical wiring can be bruised during valve surgery, and the patient may need a pacemaker for a while."
- **Level 2 (intern):** Recognizes the new wide-complex escape or AV dissociation on the monitor, connects the epicardial wires and sets a safe backup mode and rate, checks capture and sensing thresholds each shift, keeps the patient paced and AV-synchronous, orders the electrophysiology/PPM consult, and manages the practical logistics (isolation of the pacing box, anticoagulation implications).
- **Level 3 (junior resident):** Knows the incidence after isolated SAVR (roughly 3–7%, higher with heavy annular calcification, pre-existing right bundle branch block, a small or bicuspid annulus, and endocarditis with root abscess), knows the anatomic mechanism at tissue level, and knows that block appearing intraoperatively may be transient from edema and may recover over days — hence the observation window before committing to a permanent device.
- **Level 4 (senior resident):** Intraoperatively chooses suture technique to stay off the conduction axis — respects the nadir of the non-/right-coronary commissure and the membranous septum, controls the depth and angle of the subannular bites there, and decalcifies the septal annulus without gouging into it. Predicts pre-operatively which patient is high-risk and counsels them about PPM likelihood. Manages the paced patient's hemodynamics understanding that the stiff, hypertrophied, previously pressure-overloaded LV is exquisitely preload- and AV-synchrony-dependent, so ventricular-only pacing that loses atrial kick can drop cardiac output sharply.
- **Level 5 (PGY-5 exit):** All of the above, plus command of the *decision architecture*: the specific conduction patterns and their recovery probabilities, the evidence-informed timing of permanent pacemaker implantation (a typical observation window of about 5–7 days for potentially recoverable block, weighed against the cost of prolonged temporary pacing), the comparison to the TAVR conduction literature — where the permanent-pacemaker rate is markedly higher and device-dependent (about 6.5% at 30 days for the balloon-expandable valve in PARTNER 3 but roughly 20–27% for the supra-annular self-expanding valve in Evolut Low Risk) and how that higher TAVR pacemaker burden informs a lifetime-management conversation with a borderline SAVR-versus-TAVR candidate, the technical choice of pacing system in a patient who will also need durable AV synchrony, and the ability to teach the whole chain — anatomy, technique, recovery biology, and the device decision — to a junior at the bedside and defend it to an examiner.

Notice that the rejected pass's "PGY-5" objective for this topic would have sat at Level 3 at best. **That is the two-rung error, corrected.**

### Worked calibration example B — "Conduit choice in CABG"
- **Level 1:** "Surgeons use spare blood vessels — often a leg vein or a chest artery — to bypass the blockage."
- **Level 2:** Harvests the saphenous vein and preps conduit on the back table under supervision; knows the LIMA is the "best" graft and goes to the LAD; performs the Allen's test before radial harvest.
- **Level 3:** Knows LIMA-to-LAD patency exceeds ~90% at 10 years and drives the survival benefit; knows radial and BIMA are additional arterial options; can name RADIAL, ART, and the on/off-pump trials by conclusion.
- **Level 4:** Chooses the conduit strategy for the specific patient and target — assesses target quality and runoff, weighs radial versus vein against the severity of the target stenosis (competitive flow risk in a moderate lesion), factors the sternal-wound risk of BIMA in the diabetic or obese patient, and handles the conduit technically (skeletonized versus pedicled harvest, spasm prophylaxis, no-touch vein).
- **Level 5:** Designs the complete revascularization for the anatomy in front of them and defends it against the alternatives with the trial evidence at design level — the ART trial's intention-to-treat–negative result at 10 years (no BIMA survival benefit, HR ≈ 0.96) and *why* it may be negative (roughly 40% crossover from bilateral to single grafting, 22% radial use diluting the comparison, high background guideline-directed medical therapy), so that BIMA is defended on the mechanistic patency argument and the as-treated signal rather than on an intention-to-treat result that does not exist; the RADIAL pooled analysis showing the radial graft's advantage over the saphenous vein driven by fewer repeat revascularizations and better patency; the competitive-flow physiology that makes an arterial graft to a *moderately* stenosed vessel a worse choice than a vein; the inflow decisions (in-situ versus free, composite Y/T grafts, single versus double aortic clamp); the anastomotic geometry that determines patency; and the individualized lifetime-graft plan for a 52-year-old versus a 78-year-old. Knows when off-pump or a hybrid approach genuinely changes the calculus and when it is dogma.

### The ladder as an authoring discipline
For **every** topic on **every** day, write the objective at Level 5 first — the real one, the one that would survive a viva — then build the on-ramp down to Level 1 beneath it. The content between them is the day. If you cannot articulate a genuine Level 5 objective for a topic, you do not understand the topic well enough to teach it, and you must go read the source before writing. This is not a figure of speech: the authoring workflow in Part 5 makes "research the Level-5 objective before drafting anything" a literal first step, with the research protocol in 5.2 telling you where to look.

## PART 2 — THE UNIVERSAL PER-DAY CONTENT CONTRACT

Every upgraded day must satisfy this contract. It is the same shape for all fourteen days so the app's existing engines (lesson player, SRS, quiz, gating) consume the content with no special-casing, and so the depth is auditable.

### 2.1 The four-layer structure inside each day
Each day is authored in four stacked layers, gated so a learner climbs them in order. The layers map onto the five-level ladder.

1. **Orientation & Foundations (Levels 1–2).** Mechanism-first narrative prose that assumes zero retained knowledge. Defines every term at first use. One clean diagram per concept. Ends the reader at competent-clerk. This layer preserves the gentle on-ramp the app already does well — do not degrade it.
2. **Working Competence (Level 3).** The quantitative and evidentiary spine: the numbers with their conditions and exceptions, the guideline indications with class/level, the headline trials by name and conclusion, the conceptual physiology made numeric. This is where the current app mostly tops out.
3. **Senior Reasoning (Level 4).** The decision content: "why this operation for this patient," the operative flow and its variations, the intraoperative complication anticipated and managed, the ICU run for the deteriorating patient, the evidence at subgroup and controversy level. **This layer is largely absent today and is the bulk of the new writing.**
4. **PGY-5 Mastery (Level 5).** The viva-grade material: the adversarial questions and their crisp answers, the rare lethal events with dosed algorithms, the intraoperative surprises and their bailouts, the trial-design critiques, and the "when not to, when to abort, when to convert" judgment. Delivered as viva prompts, crisis runs, and the hardest MCQs. **This layer is the point of the entire rebuild.**

The four layers are not four separate documents; they are a single continuous climb the learner walks up. The transition between layers must be seamless in the prose — the Level-2 explanation of the coronary perfusion gradient becomes, three paragraphs later, the Level-4 lever the senior pulls at the bedside — so the learner experiences one escalating argument, not four disconnected tiers. But the *tagging* is discrete (every item carries its `level`), so the validator and the assessment engine can address each rung.

### 2.2 Depth requirements (the testable floors)
- **Quantitative floor.** Every threshold, dose, pressure, flow, time, and size that a senior resident is expected to know cold appears in the content *with its conditions and its exceptions* — never a naked number. "Severe AS is a mean gradient ≥ 40 mmHg" is incomplete; the content must also carry *why the gradient is flow-dependent (it varies with the square of transvalvular flow, so a failing or under-filled ventricle can generate a low gradient across a genuinely tiny valve), what a low-flow/low-gradient state does to it, and how the dobutamine challenge and the AVA/dimensionless index resolve the discordance.* Appendix C is the vetted store of these numbers-with-conditions; the author draws from it and adds to it, and never invents a figure that is not either in Appendix C or freshly sourced and cited.
- **Evidence granularity.** Every landmark trial invoked at Level 4–5 carries its population/enrollment criteria, its comparison, its primary endpoint *as defined*, the direction and rough magnitude of effect, the principal safety tradeoff, at least one methodological limitation or criticism, and how it moved (or failed to move) the guideline. Do not fabricate specific statistics; where an exact figure is not in the local source or Appendix C, state the established qualitative result and flag the number for source verification. Appendix C carries the vetted trial dossiers (population, endpoint, effect, tradeoff, criticism) for the fourteen studies the curriculum leans on; use them, and extend the format for any trial you add.
- **Operative granularity.** Every operation touched by a day is narratable step-by-step with the tissue planes, the exposure, the specific sutures/materials by type, the sizing and target numbers, the three most likely complications *at the step where they arise*, and the bailout/rescue for each. "The valve is sewn in" is a Level-1 sentence; the content must reach the bite depth, the suture technique (supra- versus intra-annular, pledgeted mattress versus continuous), and the specific hazards (annular rupture, coronary ostial obstruction, paravalvular leak, heart block) with their recognition and management.
- **Judgment content.** Every day includes at least the following Level-5 decision types where applicable: *when not to operate; when to abort or stage; when to convert (e.g., off-pump to on-pump, repair to replace); the intraoperative surprise and its rescue; the rare lethal event with its dosed, ordered algorithm.*

### 2.3 Grounding
- **Source-grounded.** Anchor to `cardiac_surgery_guide_v3.pdf` (primary), the primer, and current ACC/AHA / EACTS-ESC guideline statements. Where modern guidelines supersede the local guide, teach the current statement and note the update. Never invent statistics or trial results. The current guideline touchstones for this curriculum are the 2020 ACC/AHA Valvular Heart Disease guideline, the 2021 ACC/AHA/SCAI Coronary Revascularization guideline, and the 2022 ACC/AHA Aortic Disease guideline; when the local `cardiac_surgery_guide_v3.pdf` disagrees with these, the guideline wins and the content notes the change.
- **Reviewer-flagged uncertainty.** Any specific number, dose, or trial figure the author is not certain of is written with the established qualitative claim intact and a `<!-- VERIFY: ... -->` marker for a medical review pass. Better an honest flag than a confident fabrication. The `<!-- VERIFY -->` convention is not a weakness signal; it is the mechanism that lets the content ship *honest* while a human closes the loop. A day with ten well-placed VERIFY flags and no fabrications is stronger than a day with zero flags and three confident inventions.

### 2.4 Data shape (so nothing breaks)
Author into the existing per-day files (`lessons-dN.js`, `flashcards-dN.js`, `questions-dN.js`) and the isolated advanced modules (`js/advanced`, `js/surgery`, `js/physiology`). Keep the schemas already in `CONTENT_MANIFEST.md` and `BUILD_GUIDE.md` (lesson chunks with checkpoints; `{id, day, lesson, topic, front, back}` cards; `{id, day, lesson, topic, stem, options, answer, explain}` questions; the operation schema in `advanced-data.js`). Every new card and question carries a valid `lesson` id so gating and unlocking keep working. Run `validate.js`, `validate-anatomy.js`, `validate-trials.js`, and `validate-advanced.js` to zero errors after every day. The day-order remap already in place (Day 2 = CPB, etc.) is authoritative; author to the theme each day currently carries in `curriculum.js`.

### 2.5 The grounded quantitative floor (what "knows the number cold" actually means)
Because the naked-number tell (0.1) is the single most common way a day drifts back to the rejected level, this revision names the standard explicitly: a quantitative item is not complete until it carries **the number, the condition under which the number holds, the exception that breaks it, and the test or maneuver that resolves the exception.** All four. "FFR ≤ 0.80 defines a physiologically significant stenosis" is one-quarter of an item; the complete item also carries *the condition* (measured at maximal hyperemia, so adenosine or an equivalent must be on board and adequate), *the exception* (microvascular disease and severe LVH can uncouple FFR from the epicardial lesion, and iFR offers a hyperemia-free alternative near a 0.89 cutoff), and *the resolving move* (if the hyperemic response is doubtful, confirm with iFR or repeat with a documented adequate hyperemic stimulus). Appendix C is organized to supply exactly these four-part items; every quantitative flashcard and MCQ the author writes should be reducible to that shape.

## PART 3 — THE FIVE SEQUENCES

The rebuild runs in five ordered sequences. Sequence 1 establishes the shared standard and the measurement harness; it must complete before content work starts. Sequences 2–5 each own a band of days and carry those days' detailed PGY-5 content specifications inline. The sequences are ordered by cognitive dependency: foundations before decisions, decisions before management, management before integration.

Each sequence below states its **goal**, its **tasks**, the **per-day PGY-5 content spec** for the days it owns, and its **acceptance criteria**. The per-day specs in this revision name the specific figures the day must land; the vetted values behind them live in Appendix C, so the author cross-references rather than re-derives.

---

### SEQUENCE 1 — Calibrate, scaffold, and build the depth harness
*Owns: no days. Produces the standard everything else is measured against.*

**Goal.** Make "PGY-5 depth" a testable property before a single lesson is rewritten, so the Part-0 failure cannot recur silently.

**Tasks.**
1. **Freeze the level rubric.** Copy Part 1 of this document into a short `LEVEL_RUBRIC.md` the content agents keep open. Add the three additional worked calibration examples from Appendix A (physiology — "why EF misleads in mitral regurgitation," laddered L1→L5; critical care — "the hypotensive post-bypass patient," laddered L1→L5; evidence — "TAVR versus SAVR in the low-risk 65-year-old," laddered L1→L5). These examples are the anti-underestimation vaccine; every author reads them first. Add to `LEVEL_RUBRIC.md` the four failure tells from Part 0.1 and the before/after from Part 0.2, because recognizing the failure is as important as recognizing the target.
2. **Build the per-day competency map.** For each of the 14 days, write a one-page map listing, at each of the five levels, the specific objectives that day must deliver, using the per-day specs in Sequences 2–5 and the appropriate Appendix-C dossiers as the source. This is the contract each day's content is later audited against. Sequence 1's job is to render it into a checkable per-day checklist file; Appendix D gives the template.
3. **Build the depth validator.** Extend the validator suite with `validate-depth.js` that checks *measurable proxies* for depth per day: presence of a Level-4 "Senior Reasoning" lesson layer and a Level-5 "Mastery" set; a minimum count of decision-type items (when-not-to-operate, intraoperative-rescue, dosed-algorithm) tagged in the questions; evidence items that carry population/endpoint/limitation fields, not just a trial name; and a minimum of quantitative items with conditions attached. The validator cannot judge medical truth, but it can fail a day that is structurally shallow — no senior layer, no rescue items, naked numbers — which is exactly the shape of the rejected pass. It should also *count the four-part quantitative items* from 2.5 (number+condition+exception+resolver) and fail a day whose quantitative items are mostly one-part naked numbers.
4. **Extend the schemas minimally.** Add optional fields the depth validator reads: on questions, a `level` (1–5) and a `kind` (`recall | quantitative | evidence | operative-step | complication-rescue | when-not-to | dosed-algorithm | reconcile-discordant`); on evidence/trial references, `population`, `endpoint`, `effect`, `limitation`, and a `source` pointer into Appendix E. Keep them optional so old content still validates, but require them on all newly authored Level-4/5 items.
5. **Establish the medical-review protocol.** Every `<!-- VERIFY -->` flag is collected into a running `VERIFY_QUEUE.md` with the claim, the current best source the author found, and the specific uncertainty. A number does not ship un-flagged unless it is sourced to Appendix E or a fresh citation the author records.

**Acceptance criteria.** `LEVEL_RUBRIC.md` with five worked ladder examples plus the failure tells and before/after exists; 14 per-day competency-map checklists exist; `validate-depth.js` runs and *fails* the current (pre-upgrade) content on the days that lack a senior/mastery layer (proving it discriminates); schema fields are wired and optional; `VERIFY_QUEUE.md` exists and is wired to the authoring workflow. Only when the validator can tell shallow from deep does content work begin.

### SEQUENCE 2 — The foundation layer (Days 1–5): the cognitive substrate
*Owns: Day 1 Anatomy, Day 2 CPB & Cardioplegia, Day 3 Coronary Physiology, Day 4 Valvular Hemodynamics, Day 5 Imaging.*

**Goal.** These five days are the substrate every later decision stands on. A learner cannot reason like a PGY-5 about CABG if their coronary physiology tops out at "oxygen extraction is high," or about SAVR if their root anatomy tops out at "the valve has three leaflets." So these days must climb from a genuine novice entry to a Level-5 ceiling that is *the physiologic and anatomic reasoning a senior resident actually deploys intraoperatively.* The trap here is that "foundations" sounds like it means "shallow." It does not. The PGY-5 ceiling of an anatomy day is the operative-danger reasoning of a reoperative sternotomy; the PGY-5 ceiling of a physiology day is deriving the intervention from the pressure-volume relationship.

**Per-day PGY-5 content spec.**

**Day 1 — Cardiac & Mediastinal Anatomy.**
- *Entry (L1):* chambers, valves, great vessels, the heart on a chest film.
- *Foundations→Working (L2–3):* the coronary tree with dominance defined operationally (right-dominant in ~85–90%, left-dominant in ~7–8%, co-dominant in the rest; whoever gives the PDA supplies the inferior wall and the AV node, which is why an inferior STEMI in a right-dominant heart threatens AV nodal block, and why grafting the distal RCA/PDA matters); the grooves as surgical landmarks and the intramyocardial LAD; the conduction axis in three dimensions (SA node at the cavoatrial junction, AV node in the triangle of Koch bounded by the tendon of Todaro, the coronary sinus os and the septal tricuspid leaflet annulus, the His penetrating the central fibrous body at the membranous septum); the aortic root as a unit (virtual basal ring/annulus, three sinuses of Valsalva, coronary ostia and their heights above the annulus — typically the left main ostium ~13–15 mm and the right ~15–17 mm, with the danger being a *low* ostium under a bulky prosthesis or during root work — the sinotubular junction, the three commissures, and the fibrous aortomitral curtain continuous with the anterior mitral leaflet).
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* the three-dimensional surgical relationships — what sits immediately behind the sternum (RV free wall, and in a redo, patent grafts and a dilated aorta) and how that changes a redo entry; the transverse and oblique sinuses and how they are used for cannulation and clamping; the millimeter relationship of the circumflex to the posterior mitral annulus (the artery runs within a few millimeters of the P1 annulus in the left-dominant heart, closest at the lateral annulus, which is why deep lateral annuloplasty bites threaten it) and how left- versus right-dominance changes its course and vulnerability; the coronary ostial heights that make a low right-coronary button hazardous in root replacement; the phrenic nerve on the fibrous pericardium (and on the LIMA pedicle) and the recurrent laryngeal looping under the arch, and where each is threatened.
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* the reoperative-sternotomy hazard map read off a gated CT — RV/graft/aortic proximity to the posterior table (the danger threshold is any structure adherent to or within a few millimeters of the sternal table), the decision to expose and cannulate a femoral or axillary vessel *before* opening when the CT shows adherence, the oscillating-saw single-table-then-lift strategy, and the specific catastrophic-injury rescues (RV laceration — finger control, pack, get on peripheral bypass, repair on a decompressed heart; patent-graft avulsion — the territory it feeds and the on-pump repair; aortic entry — control, cool, arrest); the conduction-axis decisions that change suture technique in SAVR (stay superficial at the nadir of the non-/right-coronary commissure), VSD closure (buttress on the RV side away from the His), tricuspid annuloplasty (the AV node at the apex of Koch's triangle), and septal myectomy; teaching-level command of *why* the standard protective teachings are necessary but not sufficient. Viva bar: *"Reoperative sternotomy for a redo AVR in a patient with a patent LIMA-to-LAD graft — walk me through how the CT changes your plan and exactly what you do if you enter the RV on opening."*
- *Assets:* upgrade the layered anatomy diagrams; extend the surgical-danger label drill to demand the threatening maneuver *and* the rescue; 12–16 new flashcards and 10–14 questions weighted to L4–5, tagged by `kind`.

**Day 2 — Cardiopulmonary Bypass & Cardioplegia.**
- *Entry (L1):* the machine takes over heart and lungs.
- *Foundations→Working (L2–3):* the circuit drawn from memory (venous reservoir, roller vs centrifugal pump, membrane oxygenator, heat exchanger, arterial-line filter, cardioplegia delivery, LV and aortic-root vents, pump suckers, the level and bubble safety sensors); the numbers with conditions (ACT > 400–480 s before cannulation and maintained on pump, full heparinization at ~300–400 units/kg; flow index ~2.2–2.5 L/min/m² and what raises the requirement — fever, light anesthesia, inadequate hematocrit; MAP target ~50–80 mmHg and *why pressure is not perfusion*; hematocrit typically kept above ~21–24% on pump; temperature strategies from mild hypothermia ~32–34 °C to deep hypothermic circulatory arrest ~18 °C; alpha-stat vs pH-stat and *when each is chosen* — pH-stat for deep-hypothermia pediatric arrest to improve cerebral cooling, alpha-stat for routine adult work to preserve enzymatic autoregulation); antegrade vs retrograde cardioplegia and the physiologic reason retrograde protects the hypertrophied or ostially-obstructed or aortic-insufficiency heart.
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* the oxygen-delivery derivation — CaO₂ = (1.34 × Hb × SaO₂) + (0.003 × PaO₂), indexed DO₂ = CaO₂ × cardiac index × 10, the DO₂ threshold below which renal and organ injury rises (a commonly cited nadir around 270–300 mL O₂/min/m² on pump), temperature-adjusted demand, and venous saturation/lactate as the readout; myocardial protection strategy by pathology (del Nido — a 1:4 blood-to-crystalloid PlasmaLyte-A–based single dose lasting up to ~90 minutes, carrying lidocaine, magnesium, mannitol, bicarbonate and potassium; versus 4:1 blood cardioplegia re-dosed every ~15–20 minutes; the warm "hot-shot" reperfusion dose before cross-clamp removal; topical cooling; LV venting for aortic insufficiency); the troubleshooting differentials (high arterial-line pressure — cannula malposition/dissection/kink/clamp; low venous return with a rising reservoir — air lock, cannula against a wall, hypovolemia; the "won't arrest" heart — aortic insufficiency running cardioplegia into the LV, inadequate cross-clamp, delivery problem).
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* the flat or blunted arterial trace on initiating bypass as **aortic dissection until proven otherwise** — the immediate stop-pump, the re-cannulation strategy (switch to true lumen, often axillary/innominate), and the cool-and-arrest decision; the six-domain "won't come off pump" differential (residual ischemia/incomplete revascularization, an unaddressed or new valve lesion, RV failure, arrhythmia, air down the RCA, and tamponade/vasoplegia) and how TEE plus filling pressures plus direct inspection narrows it; venous air lock and massive arterial air embolism management (stop, Trendelenburg, de-air, retrograde cerebral perfusion for massive arterial air); the perfusion-emergency algorithms (oxygenator failure — hand-ventilate the oxygenator/replace; venous air; inadequate cooling). Viva bar: *"You cannot separate from bypass after a straightforward AVR — give me the ordered six-domain differential and how you distinguish the causes at the table in the next three minutes."*
- *Assets:* upgrade the CPB-circuit diagram to a clickable component map (purpose / monitored variable / failure / rescue per component); expand the oxygen-delivery physiology lab into the full perfusion cockpit with the CaO₂/DO₂ equations live; strengthen the cannulation-dissection crisis to a multi-beat run; 14–18 flashcards, 12–16 questions.

**Day 3 — Coronary Physiology & Ischemia.**
- *Entry (L1):* blocked arteries cause chest pain and infarction.
- *Foundations→Working (L2–3):* resting myocardial oxygen extraction ~70–80% (so demand is met almost entirely by raising flow, not extraction — the vulnerability that defines coronary disease); the diastolic perfusion gradient (aortic diastolic pressure − LVEDP) and why the subendocardium of the LV suffers first (it is perfused only in diastole and sits at the highest wall tension); autoregulation across a coronary pressure range and coronary flow reserve (~4–5× resting in health); the critical stenosis (~70% diameter / ~90% cross-sectional area before resting flow falls); FFR ≤ 0.80 at maximal hyperemia and iFR ≤ 0.89 as its hyperemia-free counterpart, each with the condition and exception from 2.5; collateral flow and coronary steal; the supply/demand determinants quantified (heart rate, wall tension by the Laplace relation T ∝ P·r/2h, and contractility on the demand side; FiO₂/hemoglobin/oxygen saturation, diastolic pressure and diastolic time, and patency on the supply side).
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* viability physiology — stunned (reperfused, dysfunctional but alive, recovers spontaneously) vs hibernating (chronically hypoperfused, dysfunctional, recovers *after* revascularization) vs infarcted (scar, will not recover), and how demonstrating hibernation (PET, dobutamine reserve, late-gadolinium transmurality < 50%) changes the decision to revascularize; the bedside supply/demand manipulation as an ordered set of levers with their speed of effect (slow the rate, raise the diastolic pressure, correct anemia and hypoxia, reduce wall tension); the physiology of the intra-aortic balloon pump written from the perfusion gradient (inflation in diastole raises coronary perfusion pressure; deflation in systole drops afterload and myocardial demand).
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* the ISCHEMIA-trial nuance in the specific patient — in stable disease with moderate-or-severe ischemia, an initial invasive strategy did **not** reduce all-cause mortality versus optimal medical therapy over a median 3.2 years (and the extended ISCHEMIA-EXTEND follow-up showed *lower cardiovascular* but *higher non-cardiovascular* death, netting no all-cause difference), while invasive care traded *more* periprocedural MI for *fewer* spontaneous MIs and better angina-related quality of life — and the criticisms that let a senior use it honestly (the enrollment was reduced from a planned 8000 to 5179, event rates ran lower than powered for, follow-up was modest, and left-main disease and the sickest were excluded); how all of that coexists with the *surgical survival indications* (left main, multivessel with reduced EF, diabetes with multivessel) that ISCHEMIA did not overturn; FFR/iFR-guided completeness of revascularization; and the single fastest intervention for an ischemic, tachycardic, anemic, hypotensive patient (rate control and a perfusion-pressure/oxygen-carrying fix over reflexive nitrate-driven afterload reduction that would drop the diastolic perfusion gradient) with the defense of that choice. Viva bar: *"Stable three-vessel disease, preserved EF, FFR-positive LAD — what does ISCHEMIA let you offer, what does it explicitly not change, and where is the trial vulnerable to an examiner pushing you to just medically manage everyone?"*
- *Assets:* upgrade the PV/physiology labs to include the coronary supply-demand model with an ischemic-cascade readout; 12–16 flashcards, 12–16 questions weighted to reconcile-discordant and when-not-to-revascularize.

**Day 4 — Valvular Pathology & Hemodynamics.**
- *Entry (L1):* stenosis is too tight, regurgitation leaks.
- *Foundations→Working (L2–3):* the 2020 ACC/AHA A–D staging applied to AS, AR, MS, MR; the severe-lesion thresholds with their conditions (AS: peak velocity Vmax ≥ 4 m/s, mean gradient ≥ 40 mmHg, aortic valve area ≤ 1.0 cm², dimensionless index ≤ 0.25 — all flow-dependent; severe primary MR by effective regurgitant orifice area ≥ 0.4 cm², regurgitant volume ≥ 60 mL, regurgitant fraction ≥ 50%, vena contracta ≥ 0.7 cm; severe MS mitral valve area ≤ 1.5 then very severe ≤ 1.0 cm², with the pressure-half-time caveats; the raised severe-*secondary*-MR thresholds the COAPT/MITRA-FR era produced — EROA ≥ 0.4 cm² / regurgitant volume ≥ 60 mL); pressure- vs volume-overload remodeling and the operate-before-the-ventricle-fails principle with the current intervention triggers (asymptomatic severe AS with LVEF < 50%, or LVEF ≥ 50% with a very high gradient, or a BNP > 3× normal as a IIa nudge; primary MR with LVEF ≤ 60% or LV end-systolic diameter ≥ 40 mm).
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* the pressure-volume loop of each lesion and why EF over-reads systolic function in MR and AR (the low-impedance regurgitant path lets the ventricle unload backward, inflating total stroke volume while forward output falls); the primary-vs-secondary MR distinction as two different diseases with different operations and different evidence; low-flow/low-gradient AS (classical, with low EF, and paradoxical, with preserved EF and a small hypertrophied cavity) and the dobutamine stress interpretation (a rise in stroke volume that pushes the gradient up while the valve area stays fixed ≤ 1.0 cm² = true severe with contractile reserve → operate; the valve area opening well past 1.0 cm² as flow rises = pseudo-severe → treat the myocardium); the flow-dependence of every gradient and the measurement errors that mislead.
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* placing the operative moment on the natural-history curve for a *named* patient and defending it against "wait until symptoms"; the COAPT vs MITRA-FR reconciliation — two secondary-MR transcatheter-repair trials that disagreed because COAPT enrolled *disproportionate* MR (mean EROA ~0.41 cm² with smaller ventricles, where the leak drives the heart failure and fixing it helps) while MITRA-FR enrolled *proportionate* MR (mean EROA ~0.31 cm² with larger ventricles, where the leak is a marker of a failing pump and fixing it does not) — and what that framework means for referring *this* patient; reconciling a discordant echo (small AVA but low gradient) to a management decision and the next confirmatory test (dobutamine echo, calcium score on CT) rather than a reflex threshold. Viva bar: *"Severe asymptomatic AR, LV end-systolic diameter creeping toward 50 mm, preserved EF — do you operate, on exactly which number, and what is your evidence that continuing to wait harms this specific ventricle irreversibly?"*
- *Assets:* expand the PV-loop lab to a full ventricular-mechanics lab (labelled phases, ESPVR/EDPVR, stroke work, disease presets with reverse-diagnosis mode); 14–18 flashcards, 14–18 questions.

**Day 5 — Cardiac Imaging & Interpretation.**
- *Entry (L1):* echo uses sound, cath uses dye.
- *Foundations→Working (L2–3):* the standard TEE mid-esophageal and transgastric views and what each is *for* (ME four-chamber, ME long-axis, ME aortic-valve short-axis, ME bicaval, transgastric mid-papillary and deep transgastric for the Doppler alignment that makes the AS gradient measurable); the continuity-equation derivation of AVA and why the LVOT diameter, which is *squared*, is the dominant error term; describing a coronary lesion completely (segment, %stenosis, length, calcium, TIMI flow) and the angiographic views that open each vessel; the dissection CT signs (true vs false lumen, the intimal flap and entry tear, malperfusion, Stanford A involving the ascending aorta vs B not) and the measurements that drive the operation.
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* the intraoperative pre- and post-bypass TEE checklist (baseline ventricular function and valve anatomy; post-bypass ventricular function, the valve repair/replacement result, completeness of de-airing, any new segmental wall-motion abnormality signaling a graft or air problem, paravalvular leak, and systolic anterior motion after mitral repair); reading the failing post-op heart (tamponade physiology including the localized posterior clot echo can miss, RV assessment by TAPSE and tissue-Doppler S′, LVOT obstruction after mitral repair); the measurement-error catalog (the LVOT-diameter error squared in the continuity equation, Doppler malalignment underestimating gradients, pressure recovery overestimating them in the small aorta, PISA geometric assumptions, low-flow gradients, CT motion artifact, and angiographic foreshortening).
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* driving imaging to a decision under discordance — the moderate-gradient/severe-AVA case, the next confirmatory test, and the honest limits of a static image; recognizing the intraoperative TEE finding that mandates going *back* on pump (significant residual MR after repair, SAM with a meaningful LVOT gradient, a malseated or paravalvular-leaking prosthesis) and defending the re-pump decision to a team that wants to close. Viva bar: *"Post-mitral-repair TEE shows a 40 mmHg LVOT gradient and the anterior leaflet drifting into the outflow tract in systole — what is it, what caused it, what do you try medically first, and what do you do surgically before you leave the room?"*
- *Assets:* build the annotated imaging read-out library with three-stage reveal and hotspot drills (authored SVG/illustration, licensing-clean); 12–16 flashcards, 12–16 questions weighted to reconcile-discordant.

**Sequence 2 acceptance criteria.** Each of Days 1–5 passes `validate-depth.js` with a populated Senior and Mastery layer; each carries ≥ the target counts of L4–L5 questions tagged by `kind`; the physiology labs for PV-loop, perfusion/oxygen-delivery, and coronary supply-demand are upgraded; the imaging library exists; every naked number has been given its four-part treatment (2.5) or flagged; the medical-review queue is populated and triaged. All four content validators at zero errors.

### SEQUENCE 3 — The decision and operative layer (Days 6–9)
*Owns: Day 6 Preop & Risk, Day 7 CABG, Day 8 Valve Surgery, Day 9 Aortic & Pericardial.*

**Goal.** This is where the app has the largest gap and where the rejected pass was most obviously miscalibrated. These four days must fuse disease, evidence, and operation into *"why this operation, for this patient, done this way, and defended against the alternatives."* The Level-5 ceiling here is the operating-surgeon's reasoning: the graft plan, the prosthesis choice, the cannulation strategy, the intraoperative surprise and its rescue. Each day is tightly coupled to its flagship operation module in `js/surgery`, which must be expanded in lockstep.

**Per-day PGY-5 content spec.**

**Day 6 — Preoperative Evaluation & Risk.**
- *Entry→Working (L1–3):* what "operative risk" means; STS-PROM vs EuroSCORE II — what each includes, their calibration (EuroSCORE II better calibrated than the wildly over-predicting logistic EuroSCORE I; STS trained on a large North American database and reported as a percent 30-day mortality plus morbidity endpoints), and the honest blind spots both share (frailty, porcelain aorta, hostile chest, prior chest radiation, cirrhosis); the optimization steps that change outcomes (preop anemia and iron repletion, glycemic control, smoking cessation, prehabilitation, the DAPT-interruption logistics after a recent stent, carotid disease work-up, dental clearance before valve surgery); the pre-pump workup that must be complete before the day of surgery.
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* building the case for operation — assembling the indication, quantifying risk *two* ways, listing the modifiable factors, and writing the one-paragraph "why we are operating and why now"; the frailty and futility assessment (gait speed, albumin, independence) that moves a borderline patient toward transcatheter therapy or palliation; the antithrombotic reconciliation for the specific stent type and age and the indication behind it.
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* the genuinely hard triage calls — the frail octogenarian with severe AS and a porcelain aorta (where the low STS is *misleading* because the score does not capture that cross-clamping a porcelain aorta is hazardous, so the defensible answer is transfemoral TAVR after Heart-Team review, not SAVR); the patient whose STS says low-risk but whose hostile chest, cirrhosis, or prior radiation the score does not capture; the shared-decision framing of a mechanical-vs-tissue valve in a 55-year-old who does not want lifelong anticoagulation (the durability-versus-anticoagulation tradeoff and the shrinking age threshold); and knowing when the right operation is *no* operation. Viva bar: *"Your STS says 1.8% but you are worried — name the three things the score is blind to in this patient and show me how each one changes your recommendation."*
- *Assets:* the preoperative-workbench interactive (dossier → missing tests → risk two ways → optimization → recommendation) with a transparent *teaching* risk calculator explicitly **not** masquerading as the official STS tool; 12–16 flashcards, 12–16 questions weighted to when-not-to-operate.

**Day 7 — CABG: Indications, Evidence & Operation.** (Couples to the CABG operation module.)
- *Entry→Working (L1–3):* the disease and the indication thresholds with class/level (left main, multivessel with reduced EF, and multivessel diabetes carry the strongest surgical survival recommendations); conduit science (LIMA-to-LAD patency > 90% at 10 years and the endothelial nitric-oxide biology behind arterial durability; radial vs vein; BIMA); completeness of revascularization (graft every vessel > 1.5 mm supplying viable myocardium); the evidence architecture by name and conclusion (the SYNTAX score stratifying anatomic complexity into a decision map, FREEDOM in diabetics, EXCEL and NOBLE in left main, ROOBY/CORONARY/GOPCABE on off- vs on-pump).
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* reading a coronary map and designing the operation — target quality and distal runoff, conduit-to-target matching, competitive-flow avoidance (do not put an arterial graft on a *moderately* stenosed vessel where native flow will compete and the graft will string down), sequential vs individual grafts, inflow strategy (in-situ LIMA/RIMA vs composite Y/T grafts off the LIMA, single vs double aortic clamp vs anaortic); the operative sequence narratable step-by-step with the anastomotic geometry (heel-and-toe technique, intimal-to-intimal apposition, plaque avoidance at the arteriotomy) and the numbers (ACT maintained > 400 s, flow index on pump, and acceptable transit-time flow with a low pulsatility index and good diastolic filling on the graft check).
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* defending CABG vs PCI for a *named* anatomy/comorbidity using the trials *at design level* — FREEDOM's diabetic-specific benefit (CABG reduced death/MI/stroke at 5 years, driven by fewer deaths and MIs, at the cost of *more* strokes early); the EXCEL-vs-NOBLE left-main controversy (EXCEL found PCI non-inferior on a composite but used a periprocedural-MI definition based on isolated CK-MB elevation that inflated the surgical event count and drew a formal complaint, while NOBLE, using a different MI definition, favored CABG with a primary composite of ~28% PCI vs ~19% CABG driven by more spontaneous MI and repeat revascularization after PCI — and both were industry-sponsored); the ART crossover problem (no BIMA survival benefit at 10 years by intention-to-treat, HR ≈ 0.96, but ~40% crossover and 22% radial use blur it, so the mechanistic patency case for arterial grafting survives the negative headline); the RADIAL pooled analysis favoring radial over vein with the competitive-flow caveat; and the intraoperative surprises (a porcelain aorta on opening forcing an anaortic/off-pump or alternative-inflow strategy, a poor target discovered at arteriotomy, a new territory-specific wall-motion abnormality after separation signaling a graft problem) with their rescues. Viva bar: *"Diabetic, three-vessel, SYNTAX 29, EF 40% — argue CABG over PCI with the trials at design level, then give me your graft plan and tell me exactly what you do if the ascending aorta is a rock when you go to clamp."*
- *Assets:* expand the CABG operation module to the long-form contract (14–20 steps, the numbers, complications-by-step, first-24-hours, evidence, viva); the coronary-map graft-planning interactive; 16–20 flashcards, 14–18 questions weighted to operative-step, complication-rescue, and evidence.

**Day 8 — Valve Surgery: SAVR, Mitral & Tricuspid.** (Couples to the SAVR and mitral-repair modules.)
- *Entry→Working (L1–3):* SAVR vs TAVR by age and risk (PARTNER 1 inoperable/high-risk, PARTNER 2 intermediate, PARTNER 3 low-risk balloon-expandable, Evolut Low-Risk self-expanding — with the 5-year data showing broadly similar death/stroke but a *device-dependent* pacemaker burden, ~6.5% vs surgical ~4% at 30 days for the balloon-expandable valve versus ~20–27% for the supra-annular self-expanding valve, more paravalvular leak and valve thrombosis with TAVR, and comparable early reintervention); mechanical vs tissue by age and anticoagulation willingness and the shrinking age threshold (roughly favoring mechanical below ~50 and tissue above ~65, individualized in between); structural valve degeneration and the valve-in-valve strategy; the small-annulus and bicuspid problems; degenerative MR repair science (the Carpentier classification of leaflet motion, resection vs neochordal repair, annuloplasty ring sizing, and the repair-over-replace survival and durability argument) and the SAM trap; the frequently under-treated functional tricuspid regurgitation and when to add a ring at the time of left-sided surgery.
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* narrating SAVR and degenerative mitral repair step-by-step with the hazards at each step (SAVR: annular decalcification down to healthy tissue without perforating into the LVOT or the mitral curtain, sizing to avoid patient-prosthesis mismatch — indexed EOA below ~0.85 cm²/m² moderate and below ~0.65 severe, worst in the small active or low-EF patient — the conduction-axis suture zone at the non/right commissure, coronary-ostial protection, and obsessive de-airing; mitral: systematic segmental analysis, lesion-specific repair choice, circumflex risk at the lateral annulus, and the post-repair saline test and TEE); lifetime valve planning and the coronary-access consequences of a TAVR-first strategy.
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* running a valve conference of six patients each forcing one hard call (young bicuspid AS with a dilated aorta needing concomitant aortic replacement; a small annulus forcing a root-enlargement-vs-accept-mismatch decision; ischemic secondary MR where repair durability is poor and replacement may be the honest choice; the dialysis or the pregnancy-considering patient; the asymptomatic severe primary MR with repairable anatomy where early repair beats watchful waiting) and defending each on evidence + durability + patient values; the intraoperative SAM after mitral repair — recognition on TEE, the medical temporizing (volume, afterload up, stop inotropes, slow the rate), and the surgical correction (sliding leaflet plasty or a larger ring to move the coaptation point posteriorly) with the decision to go back on pump. Viva bar: *"Fit 68-year-old, severe symptomatic AS, 21 mm annulus — TAVR or SAVR, how do you avoid patient-prosthesis mismatch, and if you open and the annulus is smaller than you sized for, what exactly do you do?"*
- *Assets:* expand the SAVR and mitral-repair modules to the long-form contract; build the valve-conference simulator; 16–20 flashcards, 16–20 questions weighted to when-not-to, operative-step, and complication-rescue.

**Day 9 — Aortic Surgery & Pericardial Disease.** (Couples to the type-A dissection module.)
- *Entry→Working (L1–3):* aneurysm thresholds (~5.5 cm for the degenerative ascending aorta, lower — ~5.0 cm or even 4.5 cm — for bicuspid/Marfan/Loeys-Dietz/family history/rapid growth > 0.5 cm per year/before pregnancy, and body-size- or height-indexed for the very small or very tall); the root-vs-supracoronary decision and valve-sparing root replacement (David reimplantation vs Yacoub remodeling); type A dissection as a surgical emergency with an untreated mortality classically cited near 1–2% *per hour* in the first 48 hours, and its malperfusion syndromes; Stanford A (ascending, virtually always operative) vs B (descending, medical unless complicated); complicated vs uncomplicated type B; tamponade physiology (pulsus paradoxus, equalized diastolic pressures, right-heart diastolic collapse on echo) and constrictive vs restrictive physiology.
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* the type-A operative strategy — cannulation choice for *antegrade true-lumen* flow (axillary/innominate or direct central true-lumen cannulation, weighing the retrograde-false-lumen and malperfusion risk of femoral return), temperature and circulatory-arrest strategy, unilateral vs bilateral antegrade cerebral perfusion, the arch extent decision (hemiarch vs total arch vs frozen elephant trunk), root management (resuspension of the commissures vs root replacement), and false-lumen obliteration; the pericardial-disease decisions (subxiphoid window vs pericardiectomy, and the constriction-vs-restriction physiology that selects the operation).
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* running the 2 a.m. type-A from ED call to sternotomy — recognizing it, imaging it (gated CT), resuscitating to permissive parameters *without delaying surgery*, choosing the cannulation site, and managing the surprises (persistent malperfusion after a technically complete proximal repair, coronary-ostial involvement needing a button or a graft, catastrophic coagulopathic bleeding from friable dissected tissue, and a new post-op neurologic deficit); the cannulation-site and arch-extent debates defended for the *specific* anatomy in front of you. Viva bar: *"Type A on CT with inferior ST elevation and a cold, pulseless right leg — what does each finding tell you about the dissection, what is your cannulation and arch plan, and what do you do if the leg malperfusion persists after you have replaced the ascending aorta and the patient is off pump?"*
- *Assets:* expand the type-A module to the long-form emergency contract; the aortic-planning-board and pericardial-physiology interactives; strengthen the dissection-on-cannulation crisis; 14–18 flashcards, 14–18 questions.

**Sequence 3 acceptance criteria.** Each of Days 6–9 passes `validate-depth.js`; the coupled operation modules (CABG, SAVR, mitral repair, type A) meet the long-form operative contract (steps, numbers, complications-by-step, first-24-hours, evidence, viva) with real operative granularity, not summaries; each day carries its interactive; the trials invoked carry population/endpoint/effect/limitation/source fields; the medical-review queue is triaged for this band. All validators at zero.

### SEQUENCE 4 — Support, hemostasis, rhythm, and critical care (Days 10–13)
*Owns: Day 10 MCS, Day 11 Hemostasis, Day 12 Pacing, Day 13 ICU & Complications.*

**Goal.** These are the management-under-uncertainty days — the ones that separate a resident who *knows about* a complication from a senior who *runs* it with a dosed, ordered algorithm at 3 a.m. The Level-5 ceiling here is the escalation ladder executed correctly under a deteriorating trace, the device matched to the failure mode, the coagulopathy resolved by targeted therapy, and the chest reopened at the bedside at the right moment. This band is where the crisis library and the bedside interactives carry most of the mastery load.

**Per-day PGY-5 content spec.**

**Day 10 — Mechanical Circulatory Support.**
- *Entry→Working (L1–3):* IABP mechanism (diastolic augmentation raising the coronary perfusion pressure, systolic deflation dropping afterload and myocardial oxygen demand, for a net cardiac-output gain of roughly half a liter per minute), the timing-error waveforms (early/late inflation and deflation), the contraindications (significant AR, aortic dissection, severe peripheral arterial disease), and the honest evidence — IABP-SHOCK II showed **no** 30-day or 6-year mortality benefit in AMI cardiogenic shock (about 66–67% mortality in both arms), which downgraded routine IABP in the guidelines; VA vs VV ECMO physiology; durable continuous-flow LVAD physiology, the INTERMACS profiles, and the complication set (RV failure after LVAD implant, driveline infection, GI bleeding from acquired von Willebrand disease and AVMs, pump thrombosis, and suction events from an underfilled LV).
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* the device-selection logic by *failure mode* — isolated LV vs RV vs biventricular vs respiratory failure — and the loading consequences (why peripheral VA-ECMO *raises* LV afterload and can distend and fail to eject the arrested LV, mandating a venting strategy; the north-south/Harlequin differential hypoxia of femoral VA-ECMO with recovering native lung and cardiac ejection; limb ischemia and the distal perfusion cannula); and the weaning strategy and its markers for each device.
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* escalating a failing heart one rung at a time (optimize preload/afterload/rhythm → inotropes → IABP → temporary VAD or VA-ECMO → durable device or transplant) with the *specific trigger* for each step and the matching of device to physiology; the VA-ECMO patient whose LV has stopped ejecting with worsening pulmonary edema — recognizing LV distension (rising PA pressures, a dilating LV on echo, pink froth), and choosing a venting approach (an Impella for "ECpella," an atrial septostomy, a surgical LV vent, or an IABP to reduce afterload); and post-LVAD RV failure recognition and management (inotropic RV support, pulmonary vasodilators, avoiding over-speeding the LVAD which worsens the septal shift). Viva bar: *"Post-cardiotomy shock, on peripheral VA-ECMO, now the LV is not ejecting and the lungs are whiting out — walk me through what is happening mechanistically and give me your venting options in order of what you would reach for first."*
- *Assets:* the MCS selection-and-escalation lab; IABP-timing and ECMO-differential-hypoxia interactives; strengthen the RV-failure crisis; 14–18 flashcards, 14–18 questions weighted to dosed-algorithm and complication-rescue.

**Day 11 — Hemostasis, Transfusion & Coagulation.**
- *Entry→Working (L1–3):* heparin dosing (~300–400 units/kg to an ACT > 400–480 s) and reversal with protamine (~1 mg per 100 units of heparin, titrated), the three protamine reaction types (Type I hypotension from fast administration, Type II anaphylactoid, and the catastrophic Type III pulmonary vasoconstriction/right-heart failure), heparin resistance and antithrombin repletion (AT concentrate or FFP); the post-bypass coagulopathy mechanisms (hemodilution, circuit-induced platelet dysfunction, hypothermia-related enzyme slowing, hyperfibrinolysis, and residual/rebound heparin) and how a viscoelastic test (TEG/ROTEM) tells them apart; transfusion thresholds and blood conservation (antifibrinolytics such as tranexamic acid, cell salvage, retrograde autologous priming).
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* the *targeted* product strategy read straight off the viscoelastic tracing (a prolonged initiation/R-time or CT → clotting factors or residual heparin, treated with FFP/PCC or more protamine; a low clot-strength/alpha-angle from fibrinogen → cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate; a low maximum amplitude from platelet dysfunction → platelets and desmopressin; an accelerated lysis/LY30 → an antifibrinolytic); the surgical-vs-coagulopathic bleeding decision with rate-based re-exploration thresholds (commonly > 400 mL/hr for 1 hour, > 300 mL/hr for 2–3 hours, or > 200 mL/hr for 4 hours, and the "sudden stop then tamponade" trap where output falling to near-zero with rising filling pressures means clot, not cure); and the bloodless-strategy plan for a patient who declines blood products.
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* running the bleeding chest as an *evolving* scenario — trending the drainage, ACT, temperature, fibrinogen, and TEG against the hemodynamics and deciding at each step whether targeted correction is still appropriate or delay has become lethal; and managing the catastrophic Type III protamine reaction as an ordered algorithm (stop protamine, support the circulation, re-heparinize and return to bypass if arriving at right-heart collapse, treat the pulmonary hypertension with inhaled pulmonary vasodilators, and protect the failing RV). Viva bar: *"Chest tubes 400 mL/hr for two hours, ACT normal, core temperature 35.2 °C, and the TEG shows a long R-time with a low alpha-angle — surgical or medical bleeding, exactly what do you give and in what order, and at what point do you take this patient back to the OR?"*
- *Assets:* the TEG/ROTEM interpreter drill; the bleeding-chest case; strengthen the protamine crisis to a multi-beat run with an RV-failure branch; 14–18 flashcards, 14–18 questions weighted to reconcile-discordant and dosed-algorithm.

**Day 12 — Pacing & Conduction Disturbances.**
- *Entry→Working (L1–3):* epicardial wire management (atrial vs ventricular wires, testing capture and sensing thresholds), the NBG pacing modes (AAI/VVI/DDD/DOO) and when each is used post-op, the sensing/capture faults (failure to capture, oversensing, undersensing) and their fixes; post-op atrial fibrillation (incidence ~20–40%, peaking around POD 2–3, the beta-blockade and amiodarone prophylaxis question, rate vs rhythm control, and the anticoagulation/stroke-risk decision if it persists).
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* programming the box to the clinical problem (failure to capture → raise the output/check the wire; oversensing → reduce the sensitivity/raise the number; complete block → DDD with an adequate backup rate to preserve AV synchrony); and heart block after AVR/mitral/septal work — the anatomy, the temporary-pacing management, and the observation window (a typical ~5–7 days) before committing to a permanent device.
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* the pacing-dependent, stiff-ventricle patient in whom losing AV synchrony collapses cardiac output — why VVI is the *wrong* mode there (it paces the ventricle but abandons the atrial kick the non-compliant hypertrophied LV depends on for filling) and DDD is right; the evidence-informed timing of permanent pacemaker implantation and the conduction patterns that predict non-recovery (persistent complete block, new persistent LBBB with a long His-ventricular interval); and the unstable rhythms (VT/VF, pause-dependent torsades, overdrive pacing) managed under a live hemodynamic trace. Viva bar: *"POD 3 after AVR, complete heart block, currently paced VVI at 70 and the pressure is 80/50 with a rising lactate — tell me precisely what is wrong with the pacing, fix it at the box, and tell me when and why you call EP for a permanent device."*
- *Assets:* the pacemaker-programmer interactive with atrial/ventricular electrograms and rhythm-strip response; rhythm-strip read-out drills; 12–16 flashcards, 12–16 questions weighted to dosed-algorithm and complication-rescue.

**Day 13 — Cardiac ICU & Postoperative Complications.**
- *Entry→Working (L1–3):* low cardiac output syndrome defined (cardiac index < 2.0–2.2 L/min/m² with a rising lactate, oliguria, and a cool, clamped-down periphery); the four Swan-Ganz hemodynamic phenotypes (hypovolemia: low CVP/PCWP, low CI; pump failure: high filling pressures, low CI, low SvO₂; vasoplegia: low SVR, high/normal CI, high SvO₂; tamponade: equalized elevated diastolic pressures) and when a Swan changes management vs when it does not; and the expected normal post-op trajectory.
- *Senior reasoning (L4):* the LCOS algorithm executed in order (preload → afterload → contractility → rate/rhythm → tamponade → ischemia → mechanical support) with the drips titrated and dosed; the vasoplegia escalation ladder with the mechanism of each rung (norepinephrine first-line; add vasopressin ~0.03–0.04 units/min for its catecholamine-sparing V1 effect; methylene blue ~1.5–2 mg/kg to inhibit the NO–cGMP pathway; hydroxocobalamin ~5 g as an NO-scavenging rescue; and angiotensin II for catecholamine-refractory vasoplegia, recognizing the evidence base is a post-hoc ATHOS-3 signal, not a dedicated cardiac-surgery trial); RV failure recognition and management; and the other-organ complications (AKI and CRRT triggers, stroke and delirium, respiratory failure, sternal wound infection and mediastinitis, mesenteric ischemia, and the post-pump systemic inflammatory response).
- *PGY-5 mastery (L5):* running the unit for the deteriorating fresh post-op — the localized posterior tamponade that a transthoracic echo can miss and the decision to reopen the chest *at the bedside* before arrest rather than after, versus escalating to mechanical support when the pump itself is the problem; distinguishing vasoplegia from ventricular failure from tamponade from surgical bleeding at the bedside when the picture is *mixed* (because real post-op shock rarely respects one box), acting on the leading diagnosis while hedging the others; and the ordered first-hour reception and deteriorating-patient protocol. Viva bar: *"Fresh post-CABG, cardiac index 1.8, CVP and PA-diastolic rising and converging, chest tubes that suddenly dropped from 200 to 20 mL/hr, and a falling pressure — what is your leading diagnosis, what confirms it fastest, and what do you do in the next five minutes?"*
- *Assets:* the ICU cockpit (handoff → verify lines/drains → trend → phenotype → action → plan) with a first-hour checklist and a deteriorating mode; the drip-titration panel with doses; strengthen the shock lab to a time-evolving cockpit; 16–20 flashcards, 16–20 questions weighted to when-not-to, dosed-algorithm, and complication-rescue.

**Sequence 4 acceptance criteria.** Each of Days 10–13 passes `validate-depth.js`; the crisis library has at least the protamine, cannulation-dissection, RV-failure, bleeding-chest, and bedside-tamponade scenarios upgraded to multi-beat runs with consequence branches and dosed debriefs; the bedside interactives (TEG, pacemaker programmer, drip titration, ICU cockpit) exist; every drip and escalation step carries its dose or is VERIFY-flagged. All validators at zero.

## PART 4 — EXECUTION, VALIDATION, AND THE ACCEPTANCE BAR

### 4.1 Order and dependencies
Run the sequences in order. Sequence 1 gates everything (no depth harness, no content work). Sequence 2 (foundations) gates Sequence 3 (the decision layer stands on the physiology and anatomy). Sequences 3 and 4 can overlap once foundations are done, but each day's coupled operation/crisis/lab must move in lockstep with its lessons so the cross-links resolve. Sequence 5 runs last because it assesses everything before it.

### 4.2 How to parallelize without losing calibration
If content is delegated to multiple agents (per the project's pod model), every agent receives `LEVEL_RUBRIC.md` and the relevant per-day competency map *first*, plus the Appendix-C dossiers for the trials and numbers its day touches, and every agent's output is run through `validate-depth.js` and a calibration spot-check against the Part-1 yardstick *before* it is accepted. The single biggest risk in parallelization is calibration drift back down toward the rejected pass's level; the rubric-first rule and the depth validator are the guardrails against it. Do **not** accept an agent's day because it is long or well-written — accept it only when its Level-5 layer would survive a viva, tested by literally asking the day's viva-bar question and checking that the day's own content answers it three levels deep.

### 4.3 The depth audit as a standing gate
`validate-depth.js` runs after every day and in CI-equivalent form after every sequence. A day is not merged if it lacks a populated Senior and Mastery layer, the minimum L4–L5 question counts by `kind`, evidence items with population/endpoint/limitation/source, and quantitative items with the four-part treatment from 2.5. This is deliberately the structural signature of *not* being the rejected pass.

### 4.4 What "done" means
The rebuild is complete when, for all 14 days: (1) the content spans a genuine Level-1 entry to a genuine Level-5 exit as defined in Part 1; (2) `validate-depth.js` and all four content validators pass at zero; (3) the `VERIFY_QUEUE.md` is empty or every number is sourced to Appendix E or a recorded citation; (4) the coupled operations, labs, and crises meet their contracts; (5) Day 14's multi-format assessment can actually test the Level-5 bar; and (6) a human medical reviewer has signed that each day's mastery layer clears the "could a well-read fourth-year medical student say this after one chapter?" test with a clear *no.*

### 4.5 The one-sentence standard to keep on the wall
**If a strong, interested medical student could say your "PGY-5" sentence after reading one good chapter, it is not a PGY-5 sentence — the real one sits two levels above it, in operative judgment, quantitative intraoperative reasoning, trial-design command, or the dosed management of a rare lethal event.** Everything in this plan exists to enforce that sentence.

## PART 5 — THE AUTHORING WORKFLOW (how Claude actually produces one day)

Parts 0–4 define the *standard*. This part defines the *motion* — the exact, repeatable sequence the content agent runs to take a single day from its current shallow state to a validated PGY-5 exit. It exists because the rejected pass did not fail for lack of a standard; it failed because the author drafted from memory at whatever level came naturally and labeled the result "PGY-5." The countermeasure is a workflow that forces research before drafting, drafts top-down from the Level-5 objective, and self-audits against the failure tells before anything is accepted. Run this loop once per day, in order.

### 5.1 Step 0 — Load the calibration into working context
Before touching a day, re-read (in this order): the Part-0 failure tells and before/after, the Part-1 ladder and the relevant worked example, the day's own competency map from Sequence 1, and the Appendix-C dossiers for every trial and number the day will invoke. Do not skip this because the day "looks familiar." Calibration is not durable across days; it must be reloaded, because the drift back toward the interested-student level is silent and happens precisely when the author feels confident.

### 5.2 Step 1 — Research the Level-5 objectives (before drafting a single sentence)
For each topic the day owns, write the **Level-5 objective first** (per Part 1's authoring discipline). If you cannot state it precisely from your own command of the material, you research it before you draft anything else. The research protocol, in order of preference:
1. **The local sources first** — `cardiac_surgery_guide_v3.pdf`, the primer, and the existing per-day files — for the app's own framing and any figures already vetted.
2. **Appendix C of this document** for the pre-verified trial dossiers and numbers-with-conditions.
3. **Current guideline statements** — the 2020 ACC/AHA VHD guideline, the 2021 ACC/AHA/SCAI revascularization guideline, the 2022 ACC/AHA aortic-disease guideline, and the corresponding ESC/EACTS documents — for any indication, class, or threshold, because these supersede the local guide where they disagree.
4. **The primary trial literature and PubMed** for any effect size, endpoint definition, or criticism you intend to state at Level 4–5. Prefer the trial's own publication and major secondary analyses over summaries.
5. **Live web search** for anything that may have moved since this document was written (July 2026) — a superseding guideline, a new long-term follow-up, a changed threshold. When you find a newer authoritative figure than Appendix C carries, use the newer one and note the update in `VERIFY_QUEUE.md` so Appendix C can be refreshed.

The output of Step 1 is, per topic: the written Level-5 objective, the two or three numbers it hinges on (each in the four-part form from 2.5), and the one or two trials it leans on (each in the Appendix-C dossier form). Any figure you could not verify is written now, at research time, with its `<!-- VERIFY -->` note, not patched in later.

### 5.3 Step 2 — Build the on-ramp down to Level 1
With the Level-5 objective fixed, write *downward*: the Level-4 senior-reasoning content that a learner needs to reach the L5 objective, then the Level-3 working spine beneath that, then the Level-1–2 orientation that makes the whole climb credible to a novice. Writing top-down is not stylistic; it is the mechanism that prevents the day from topping out early. If you write bottom-up, you stop when you run out of easy material — which is exactly at the interested-student level. Writing from the L5 objective down forces every lower layer to *serve* the summit.

### 5.4 Step 3 — Author the practice items to the exemplar register
Write the flashcards and MCQs to the Appendix-B register — judgment, sequence, numbers-with-conditions, discordance, and rescue, never naked recall — hitting the day's target counts and `kind` distribution. Every Level-4/5 item carries its `level`, `kind`, and (for evidence items) the `population/endpoint/effect/limitation/source` fields. A useful discipline: for each item, name which failure tell (0.1) it would exhibit if written lazily, then confirm the item avoids it. A "what is the indication for X" recall card is the naked-number or name-drop tell in card form; rewrite it into a decision.

### 5.5 Step 4 — Wire the coupled assets
Move the day's operation module, crisis run, and physiology/imaging lab in lockstep so the cross-links resolve. An operation module meets the long-form contract only when it is *narratable* (14–20 steps with tissue planes, sutures, and numbers), *complication-mapped* (the three likeliest complications at the step where they arise, each with a rescue), and *evidence-anchored* (the trials that justify the operation, in dossier form). A crisis run meets the bar only when it is *multi-beat* (choices have consequences that change the next beat), *dosed* (every drug and escalation carries its dose), and *debriefed* (each wrong path links to the exact lesson that repairs it).

### 5.6 Step 5 — Self-audit against the failure tells, then validate
Before running the validator, read the day's Level-4/5 layers against the four failure tells from Part 0.1 and answer, in writing, the day's own viva-bar question using only the content the day contains. If the content cannot answer its own viva question three levels deep, the day is not done regardless of length. Then run `validate.js`, `validate-anatomy.js`, `validate-trials.js`, `validate-advanced.js`, and `validate-depth.js` to zero errors, triage any new `<!-- VERIFY -->` flags into `VERIFY_QUEUE.md`, and only then submit the day for the human calibration spot-check. Length is never evidence of depth; the ability to survive the day's own viva is.

### 5.7 The loop, compressed
Load calibration → research and write the L5 objective (with sourced numbers and trial dossiers) → build the on-ramp downward → author items to the exemplar register → wire the coupled assets → self-audit against the failure tells and answer the day's viva question → validate to zero and triage VERIFY flags → human spot-check. Repeat per day. Never draft before researching; never accept before the day answers its own viva.

## APPENDIX A — THE THREE ADDITIONAL WORKED CALIBRATION LADDERS

Sequence 1 requires `LEVEL_RUBRIC.md` to carry, alongside the two ladders in Part 1 (heart block after AVR; conduit choice in CABG), three more worked examples spanning physiology, critical care, and evidence. They are written out here in full so the rubric can be assembled directly and so every author sees, before writing a word, exactly how far "PGY-5" is from "interested student" across all three registers. These are the anti-underestimation vaccine — study them until the two-rung gap is reflexive.

### Ladder C (physiology) — "Why the ejection fraction misleads in mitral regurgitation"
- **Level 1 (med student):** "A leaky mitral valve lets blood go backward, so the heart has to pump more." Recognizes the holosystolic murmur radiating to the axilla and knows MR causes volume overload.
- **Level 2 (intern):** Knows that in chronic MR the EF is often reported as "preserved" or even high, and that the patient can still be in trouble; can present the echo report and flag a dilating ventricle.
- **Level 3 (junior resident):** Explains *why* the EF over-reads systolic function: the regurgitant orifice is a low-impedance path, so the ventricle unloads part of its stroke volume backward into the low-pressure left atrium during systole. The total (angiographic) stroke volume is large, so EF looks fine, but the *forward* (effective) stroke volume — what reaches the body — is falling. Knows that the operative-window triggers therefore rely on chamber size and the LV end-systolic dimension, not on EF alone, and that an EF that has drifted down toward 60% in severe primary MR already signals meaningful contractile injury (hence the current LVEF ≤ 60% and LVESD ≥ 40 mm triggers).
- **Level 4 (senior resident):** Reasons it on the pressure-volume loop: MR abolishes true isovolumic contraction because the mitral valve is incompetent from the moment LV pressure rises, so the loop loses its vertical left border and shifts leftward as the LV empties into the LA before the aortic valve opens. Afterload is deceptively low (the LV ejects part of its volume against LA pressure, not aortic pressure), which flatters both EF and the apparent contractility; correcting the MR *raises* afterload and can unmask a weak ventricle post-repair. Uses this to counsel that a "normal" EF pre-op does not guarantee a normal EF after the leak is closed, and to set the operate-early threshold in asymptomatic severe primary MR with repairable anatomy.
- **Level 5 (PGY-5 exit):** Integrates the loop physiology into the operative and peri-operative plan: predicts the post-repair afterload-mismatch drop in EF and pre-positions inotropic and afterload-reducing support; distinguishes this primary-MR physiology from secondary/functional MR where the ventricle is the primary problem and closing the valve does not fix the pump (the COAPT-vs-MITRA-FR reconciliation, disproportionate vs proportionate MR); recognizes on the post-bypass TEE the difference between a competent repair with a transiently stunned ventricle and a failing repair, and defends the decision to accept, revise, or replace. Can derive the whole chain — regurgitant fraction, effective vs total stroke volume, the loop geometry, the afterload transfer at repair — at a whiteboard for a junior and defend the timing of surgery to an examiner three levels deep.

### Ladder D (critical care) — "The hypotensive patient two hours out from bypass"
- **Level 1 (med student):** "Their blood pressure is low; they might be bleeding or the heart might be weak; call for help." Knows the vital signs and that this is an emergency.
- **Level 2 (intern):** Runs the immediate reflex — check the arterial line and cuff for accuracy, look at the CVP and the chest-tube output, ensure the pacing wires are capturing and AV-synchronous, give a small fluid bolus, and escalate to the senior/attending. Can start a pressor at a starting dose and call the values out.
- **Level 3 (junior resident):** Frames it as the ordered low-cardiac-output differential — preload, afterload, contractility, rate/rhythm, tamponade, ischemia, mechanical — and reads the Swan-Ganz profile to localize it (low CVP/PCWP and low CI = hypovolemia; high filling pressures with low CI = pump failure; low SVR with high CI = vasoplegia; equalized diastolic pressures = tamponade). Titrates the appropriate agent to the identified problem rather than reflexively.
- **Level 4 (senior resident):** Recognizes the *mixed* and the *deceptive* pictures: the localized posterior clot that tamponades the LA or RA with chest tubes that have suddenly gone quiet and an echo that can look unimpressive; the RV failure with a high CVP but an underfilled LV; the post-CPB vasoplegia that mimics hypovolemia until the SVR is measured. Executes the vasoplegia escalation ladder with mechanism (norepinephrine → vasopressin → methylene blue → hydroxocobalamin → angiotensin II) and knows when a Swan is misleading rather than diagnostic. Decides when the answer is volume and when volume is the trap.
- **Level 5 (PGY-5 exit):** Makes and owns the reopen-the-chest-at-the-bedside decision — the specific triggers (sudden drop in previously brisk drainage with rising and converging filling pressures and falling CI, refractory to the algorithm), the sterile bedside reopening, and the judgment to do it *before* arrest rather than after; distinguishes at the bedside, under a mixed and evolving trace, tamponade from RV failure from vasoplegia from surgical bleeding and acts on the leading diagnosis while hedging the others; escalates to mechanical support at the right rung when the pump is the problem; and debriefs the team on which signals were discriminating and which were noise. Viva-grade: can defend the reopen decision, or the decision *not* to reopen, against an examiner pushing both ways.

### Ladder E (evidence) — "TAVR versus SAVR in the low-risk 65-year-old"
- **Level 1 (med student):** "TAVR replaces the valve with a catheter instead of open surgery; it avoids a big incision." Knows TAVR exists and is less invasive.
- **Level 2 (intern):** Knows TAVR was first for high-risk/inoperable patients and has expanded to lower risk; can present the patient's STS score and the Heart Team's plan.
- **Level 3 (junior resident):** Names PARTNER 3 and Evolut Low-Risk and their headline conclusion (TAVR non-inferior — and on some short-term composites favorable — to SAVR in selected low-risk patients), and knows the durability and lifetime-management arguments that keep SAVR in play for the young.
- **Level 4 (senior resident):** Commands the trials at design level — the enrollment criteria (age skew with a mean in the early-to-mid 70s even in the "low-risk" trials, so the data are thin for a *true* 65-year-old; the exclusion of bicuspid anatomy; the anatomic CT screening), the primary endpoint *as composited* (death/stroke/rehospitalization) and its follow-up horizon (short-term composites do not settle 10–15-year durability), the device-dependent pacemaker rate and the paravalvular-leak and valve-thrombosis tradeoffs of TAVR at 5 years, and the coronary-access and valve-in-valve consequences of putting a transcatheter valve in first. Uses this to frame a genuine lifetime-management conversation rather than defaulting to "less invasive is better."
- **Level 5 (PGY-5 exit):** Individualizes and defends the decision for *this* 65-year-old against adversarial questioning: weighs the durability uncertainty and the future re-intervention pathway (TAVR-in-TAVR feasibility, coronary re-access after a supra-annular device, the annular anatomy that forecloses options), the bicuspid and small-annulus caveats the trials excluded, the pacemaker and PVL burden against the sternotomy and its recovery, and the patient's own values — and can argue the *opposite* choice equally well, because at this level the answer is "it depends, and here is exactly on what." Knows where each trial is vulnerable and what the next generation of evidence still has to prove.

The through-line across all five ladders (A–E): the Level-5 rung is never a *fact* — it is a *defended decision under adversarial questioning, grounded in mechanism, evidence at design level, and the exception.* Author every day's mastery layer to that rung.

## APPENDIX B — MODEL EXEMPLAR ITEMS (the register the new cards and questions must hit)

To stop authors from drifting back to recall items, here are model flashcards and MCQs at the Level-4/5 bar, tagged by `kind`. Every day's new practice set should contain items of this character, not "what is the indication for X" recall.

**Flashcard (kind: operative-step), Day 8 SAVR —**
Front: "During SAVR you have opened the aorta and antegrade cardioplegia is now running poorly with the ventricle starting to distend. Why, and what do you switch to?"
Back: "Once the aortotomy is open, antegrade root cardioplegia leaks out through the incompetent/excised valve instead of pressurizing the coronaries, so myocardial protection fails and the LV warms and distends. Switch to direct ostial antegrade cannulae into the coronary ostia and/or retrograde delivery via the coronary sinus, and vent the LV. The one-line why: an open, incompetent aortic root cannot hold a column of cardioplegia."

**Flashcard (kind: complication-rescue), Day 7 CABG —**
Front: "Two hours post-CABG (LIMA-LAD, SVG to OM and PDA) the monitor shows new ST elevation in II/III/aVF and the pressure drops. Most likely mechanism and next step?"
Back: "An early *territory-specific* change points to a graft problem in that territory — here the RCA/PDA graft (air down the graft, kink/tension, or acute occlusion) — not to diffuse ischemia. The move is to interrogate and revise (echo for the new inferior wall-motion abnormality, return to the OR, graft interrogation), not to medicate around it with fluid or pressor alone. Air down a fresh RCA graft classically stuns the inferior wall and recovers, but you do not assume that until you have excluded a mechanical graft failure."

**MCQ (kind: reconcile-discordant, level 5), Day 4 —**
Stem: "A 78-year-old with an aortic valve area of 0.9 cm² but a mean gradient of 28 mmHg and an EF of 35%. Dobutamine echo raises the mean gradient to 46 mmHg with the AVA rising only to 1.0 cm² and a rise in stroke volume. The best interpretation and next step is:"
Options: (a) true severe low-flow/low-gradient AS with contractile reserve — proceed toward AVR; (b) pseudo-severe AS — the valve opens with more flow, treat the cardiomyopathy medically; (c) moderate AS, defer; (d) measurement error, repeat in three months.
Answer: (a). Explain: with contractile reserve (a rise in stroke volume on dobutamine) the gradient climbs while the valve area stays fixed near 1.0 cm² or below, which unmasks *true* severe AS driving the low-EF state; the presence of reserve also predicts better operative survival. Pseudo-severe AS would show the AVA opening well beyond 1.0 cm² as flow rises with little gradient change. This is exactly the discordance a senior resident must resolve rather than read one number.

**MCQ (kind: dosed-algorithm, level 5), Day 13 —**
Stem: "A fresh post-CABG patient has CI 1.6, MAP 55, SVR 480 dyn·s·cm⁻⁵, CVP 8, PCWP 10, SvO₂ 72%, and a rising lactate, already on norepinephrine 0.2 µg/kg/min. The physiology and best next agent are:"
Options: (a) vasoplegia — add vasopressin, then methylene blue if refractory; (b) hypovolemia — give 2 L crystalloid; (c) LV failure — start an inotrope and IABP; (d) tamponade — reopen the chest.
Answer: (a). Explain: low SVR with a *high-normal* SvO₂ and low-normal filling pressures is distributive (vasoplegic) shock, not pump failure (which would show high filling pressures and a low SvO₂) or tamponade (equalized, elevated filling pressures). The escalation is vasopressin next (~0.03–0.04 units/min) for its catecholamine-sparing V1 effect, then methylene blue (~1.5–2 mg/kg) and hydroxocobalamin (~5 g) for refractory vasoplegia by inhibiting/scavenging the NO–cGMP pathway. Flooding this patient with volume or reflexively reopening the chest treats the wrong diagnosis.

**MCQ (kind: when-not-to, level 5), Day 6 —**
Stem: "An 84-year-old with severe symptomatic AS, a heavily calcified 'porcelain' ascending aorta on CT, gait speed of 0.6 m/s, and an albumin of 2.9. STS-PROM returns 2.1%. The most defensible recommendation is:"
Options: (a) transfemoral TAVR after Heart Team review; (b) SAVR because the STS is low; (c) balloon valvuloplasty as destination therapy; (d) medical management only.
Answer: (a). Explain: the low STS is misleading because the score does not capture porcelain aorta (which makes cross-clamping the ascending aorta hazardous), frailty (slow gait speed), or nutritional depletion — precisely the factors that move a "low-risk-by-score" patient away from open SAVR toward a transcatheter option. Recognizing that a low STS does not equal a low true risk, and naming *which* uncaptured factors drive the decision, is the senior-level judgment being tested.

**MCQ (kind: evidence, level 5), Day 7 — (new in this revision, to model the evidence `kind` at full dossier depth)**
Stem: "You are defending CABG over PCI for a diabetic with three-vessel disease and are asked why you weight FREEDOM so heavily and where it is vulnerable. The strongest single answer is:"
Options: (a) FREEDOM randomized diabetics with multivessel disease and showed CABG reduced the composite of death/MI/stroke at 5 years, driven by fewer deaths and MIs, at the cost of more early stroke — but it predates current-generation drug-eluting stents and complete-revascularization PCI technique, which is its main vulnerability; (b) FREEDOM proved CABG is better for all coronary disease; (c) FREEDOM was a registry, so it only shows association; (d) FREEDOM favored PCI on quality of life, so the choice is a wash.
Answer: (a). Explain: the defensible use of FREEDOM is precise — its population (diabetics, multivessel), its endpoint (a composite driven by death and MI), its tradeoff (early stroke excess with CABG), and its honest limitation (stent-generation and technique drift since enrollment). Stating the trial that way is Level 5; "CABG is just better" is the name-drop tell.

**Flashcard (kind: dosed-algorithm), Day 11 Hemostasis — (new in this revision)**
Front: "Immediately after protamine the MAP collapses, the PA pressure spikes, and the RV distends and fails on TEE. What is this and what is your ordered response?"
Back: "A Type III (catastrophic) protamine reaction — protamine-induced pulmonary vasoconstriction causing acute RV failure. Ordered response: stop the protamine; support the circulation (epinephrine, restore coronary perfusion pressure); if arriving at RV collapse, re-heparinize and return to bypass to unload the RV; treat the pulmonary hypertension with an inhaled pulmonary vasodilator (inhaled nitric oxide or prostacyclin); and protect/offload the RV. The one-line why: protamine can be a pulmonary vasoconstrictor, and the RV — not the lungs — is what kills the patient in the next two minutes."

Author every day's new set to this register: judgment, sequence, numbers-with-conditions, discordance, and rescue — never naked recall.

## APPENDIX C — THE GROUNDED EVIDENCE & NUMBERS VAULT

This appendix is new in revision 2.0 and is the single most important addition. It exists so that no author ever has to reconstruct a landmark figure from memory and no day ever ships a fabricated statistic. Every dossier and number below was checked against a current source at the time of writing (July 2026); the sources are listed in Appendix E and keyed by the bracketed tags. **Use these as the verified anchors.** Where a day needs a figure not in this vault, the author sources it fresh (per the Part-5 research protocol), records the citation, and — ideally — adds a new dossier here in the same format so the vault grows. Where any figure below has plausibly moved since July 2026, the author re-verifies at authoring time and updates it. The format for trials is fixed: **Population · Comparison · Primary endpoint (as defined) · Effect (direction and rough magnitude) · Principal tradeoff · Key limitation/criticism · Guideline effect.**

### C.1 Trial dossiers

**PARTNER 3 (low-risk TAVR, balloon-expandable).** [P3]
- *Population:* Low-surgical-risk severe symptomatic AS; mean age ~73; tricuspid anatomy; excluded bicuspid.
- *Comparison:* Transfemoral balloon-expandable TAVR vs SAVR.
- *Primary endpoint:* Composite of death, stroke, or rehospitalization at 1 year.
- *Effect:* TAVR superior at 1 year on the composite; by 5 years death and stroke were similar between arms.
- *Tradeoff:* At 30 days, permanent pacemaker ~6.5% (TAVR) vs ~4.0% (SAVR); more paravalvular leak with TAVR; valve thrombosis ~2.5% (TAVR) vs ~0.2% (SAVR) over follow-up.
- *Limitation:* Short follow-up relative to the durability question in young patients; bicuspid excluded; mean age well above a "true" low-risk 60s patient.
- *Guideline effect:* Supported extending TAVR to low-risk patients with shared decision-making and Heart-Team/lifetime-management framing.

**Evolut Low Risk (low-risk TAVR, self-expanding supra-annular).** [ELR]
- *Population:* Low-risk severe symptomatic AS; self-expanding supra-annular device.
- *Comparison:* Self-expanding TAVR vs SAVR.
- *Primary endpoint:* Death or disabling stroke at 24 months (Bayesian non-inferiority), extended to 5 years.
- *Effect:* TAVR non-inferior to SAVR through 5 years for death/disabling stroke.
- *Tradeoff:* Substantially higher permanent-pacemaker burden — roughly 20–27% cumulative with the self-expanding valve vs ~11% surgical, most within the first two years; more paravalvular leak.
- *Limitation:* Device-specific pacemaker signal much higher than the balloon-expandable valve; durability beyond 5 years still open.
- *Guideline effect:* Reinforced device-dependent counseling — the pacemaker tradeoff differs markedly by valve platform.

**FREEDOM (CABG vs PCI in diabetes).** [FRE]
- *Population:* Diabetics with multivessel coronary disease.
- *Comparison:* CABG vs PCI with drug-eluting stents.
- *Primary endpoint:* Composite of all-cause death, MI, or stroke at ~5 years (median follow-up ~3.8 years, minimum 2).
- *Effect:* CABG superior — lower composite driven by fewer deaths and MIs.
- *Tradeoff:* Higher early stroke rate with CABG.
- *Limitation:* Enrolled before current-generation DES and contemporary complete-revascularization/physiology-guided PCI; generalizability to today's stents debated.
- *Guideline effect:* Anchors the strong recommendation for CABG in multivessel diabetic disease.

**EXCEL and NOBLE (left main revascularization).** [EXC/NOB]
- *Population:* Left main disease, largely low-to-intermediate anatomic complexity.
- *Comparison:* PCI vs CABG.
- *Primary endpoint:* EXCEL — composite of death/stroke/MI (with a controversial periprocedural-MI definition based partly on isolated CK-MB elevation); NOBLE — composite of death, non-procedural MI, repeat revascularization, and stroke.
- *Effect:* EXCEL found PCI non-inferior to CABG; NOBLE favored CABG (primary composite ~28% PCI vs ~19% CABG), driven by more spontaneous MI and repeat revascularization after PCI.
- *Tradeoff / controversy:* EXCEL's periprocedural-MI definition inflated the surgical event count and drew a formal complaint that the protocol's universal-definition analysis was not reported; both trials were industry-sponsored (Abbott for EXCEL, Biosensors for NOBLE).
- *Limitation:* Divergent MI definitions largely explain the divergent conclusions; complexity-limited populations.
- *Guideline effect:* Contributed to the more cautious, complexity-stratified left-main recommendations and the downgrading of the PCI class of recommendation in some documents.

**ISCHEMIA and ISCHEMIA-EXTEND (stable ischemic heart disease).** [ISC]
- *Population:* Stable coronary disease with moderate-to-severe ischemia on stress testing; left main excluded by blinded CT.
- *Comparison:* Initial invasive strategy (angiography + revascularization + OMT) vs initial conservative (OMT alone, invasive if OMT fails).
- *Primary endpoint:* Composite of CV death, MI, resuscitated arrest, or hospitalization for unstable angina/heart failure.
- *Effect:* No significant reduction in the primary composite or in all-cause mortality over a median 3.2 years; invasive care improved angina-related quality of life. Extended follow-up (ISCHEMIA-EXTEND) showed *lower CV death* but *higher non-CV death* with invasive, netting no all-cause mortality difference over ~5.7 years; invasive traded more periprocedural MI for fewer spontaneous MIs.
- *Tradeoff:* Early procedural harm (periprocedural MI) vs later spontaneous-MI reduction.
- *Limitation:* Enrollment cut from a planned 8000 to 5179; lower-than-expected event rates; modest follow-up for a mortality question; excluded left main and the sickest.
- *Guideline effect:* Reinforced OMT-first for *stable* disease with preserved EF, without overturning the survival indications (left main, multivessel + low EF, diabetes + multivessel).

**COAPT vs MITRA-FR (transcatheter repair of secondary MR).** [CO/MF]
- *Population:* Heart failure with secondary MR on guideline-directed medical therapy. COAPT: more severe MR relative to LV size (disproportionate), mean EROA ~0.41 cm², smaller LV volumes. MITRA-FR: less severe MR relative to a more dilated LV (proportionate), mean EROA ~0.31 cm².
- *Comparison:* Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (MitraClip) + GDMT vs GDMT alone.
- *Primary endpoint:* COAPT — heart-failure hospitalizations at 24 months; MITRA-FR — death or HF hospitalization at 12 months.
- *Effect:* COAPT positive (fewer HF hospitalizations and lower mortality); MITRA-FR neutral.
- *Reconciliation:* The proportionate/disproportionate framework — where MR is the dominant lesion relative to LV dilation (COAPT), fixing it helps; where MR is a marker of an already-failing dilated ventricle (MITRA-FR), it does not.
- *Guideline effect:* Raised the severe-secondary-MR thresholds (EROA ≥ 0.4 cm² / regurgitant volume ≥ 60 mL) and restricted the transcatheter indication toward COAPT-like anatomy on optimized GDMT.

**ART (bilateral vs single internal mammary grafting).** [ART]
- *Population:* Patients undergoing CABG, symptomatic multivessel disease.
- *Comparison:* Bilateral internal mammary artery grafting vs single.
- *Primary endpoint:* All-cause mortality at 10 years.
- *Effect:* No significant difference by intention-to-treat (HR ≈ 0.96, 95% CI ~0.82–1.12).
- *Tradeoff:* More sternal-wound complications with BIMA, especially in diabetics.
- *Limitation:* ~40% crossover from bilateral to single grafting, 22% radial-artery use, and high background guideline-directed medical therapy all dilute the comparison — so the negative intention-to-treat result does not cleanly refute the mechanistic patency case for multiple arterial grafts.
- *Guideline effect:* Kept multi-arterial grafting a reasonable, individualized choice rather than a mandate; heightened attention to sternal-wound risk selection.

**RADIAL pooled analysis (radial artery vs saphenous vein as a second conduit).** [RAD]
- *Population:* Patients receiving a second conduit alongside the LIMA.
- *Comparison:* Radial artery graft vs saphenous vein graft.
- *Effect:* Radial associated with better patency and fewer adverse cardiac events / repeat revascularizations over follow-up.
- *Caveat:* Benefit depends on a *severely* stenosed target — an arterial graft to a moderately stenosed vessel risks competitive flow and graft failure.
- *Guideline effect:* Supported a IIa-level preference for radial over vein for a suitably stenosed non-LAD target.

**IABP-SHOCK II (intra-aortic balloon pump in AMI cardiogenic shock).** [IABP]
- *Population:* Acute MI complicated by cardiogenic shock, planned early revascularization.
- *Comparison:* IABP vs no IABP.
- *Primary endpoint:* 30-day all-cause mortality.
- *Effect:* No difference (~40% 30-day mortality both arms in the trial; ~66–67% at 6-year follow-up), no benefit at long-term follow-up.
- *Guideline effect:* Downgraded routine IABP in AMI cardiogenic shock; shifted device use toward selected cases and other platforms.

### C.2 Numbers-with-conditions (the four-part quantitative store)

Each entry below is written to the 2.5 standard — number · condition · exception · resolver — so it can be dropped into a flashcard or MCQ directly.

- **Severe AS gradient.** Mean ≥ 40 mmHg / Vmax ≥ 4 m/s / AVA ≤ 1.0 cm² · holds at normal transvalvular flow · breaks in low-flow states (low EF or small hypertrophied cavity), where a genuinely severe valve can show a low gradient · resolved by the dimensionless index (≤ 0.25), the AVA, a CT calcium score, and a dobutamine challenge to separate true-severe (gradient rises, AVA stays ≤ 1.0) from pseudo-severe (AVA opens past 1.0). [VHD]
- **Severe primary MR.** EROA ≥ 0.4 cm², regurgitant volume ≥ 60 mL, regurgitant fraction ≥ 50%, vena contracta ≥ 0.7 cm · by integrated quantitative echo · the PISA method's geometric assumptions err with eccentric or multiple jets · resolved by integrating multiple parameters and CMR regurgitant-fraction quantification. [VHD]
- **Primary MR intervention triggers.** LVEF ≤ 60% or LV end-systolic diameter ≥ 40 mm in asymptomatic severe primary MR · because EF over-reads systolic function through the low-impedance leak · earlier repair is favored when the anatomy is repairable and the surgical risk low · confirmed by serial imaging and the feasibility-of-repair assessment. [VHD]
- **FFR / iFR.** FFR ≤ 0.80 / iFR ≤ 0.89 defines a physiologically significant epicardial lesion · FFR requires adequate maximal hyperemia · microvascular disease and severe LVH uncouple FFR from the epicardial stenosis · resolved by ensuring adequate hyperemia or using the hyperemia-free iFR. [REV]
- **del Nido cardioplegia.** 1:4 blood-to-crystalloid, PlasmaLyte-A base, single dose lasting up to ~90 minutes · in an adult heart with reasonable cross-clamp times and without severe LVH · very long cross-clamp or heavy hypertrophy prompts a re-dose before 90 minutes · resolved by monitoring for electrical activity and re-dosing on return of activity. [DN]
- **On-pump targets.** ACT > 400–480 s before/on bypass; flow index ~2.2–2.5 L/min/m²; MAP ~50–80 mmHg; hematocrit kept above ~21–24% · at mild-to-moderate hypothermia · demand rises with fever/light anesthesia and falls with deeper hypothermia · resolved by tracking SvO₂, lactate, and indexed DO₂ (nadir tolerated ~270–300 mL/min/m²) rather than pressure alone. [CPB]
- **Ascending aortic surgical thresholds.** ~5.5 cm degenerative; ~5.0 cm (or lower) for bicuspid/heritable; growth > 0.5 cm/year; indexed for body size · these are elective thresholds · a dissection or symptoms override the size number entirely · resolved by heritable-syndrome-specific thresholds and cross-sectional-area-to-height indexing in the small or tall patient. [AO]
- **Post-op re-exploration for bleeding.** > 400 mL/hr ×1 h, > 300 mL/hr ×2–3 h, or > 200 mL/hr ×4 h · with a corrected coagulopathy · a sudden fall in previously brisk output with rising filling pressures signals tamponade, not resolution · resolved by TEG/ROTEM-guided targeted correction first and echo for clot. [HEM]
- **Vasoplegia ladder doses.** Norepinephrine first-line → vasopressin ~0.03–0.04 units/min → methylene blue ~1.5–2 mg/kg → hydroxocobalamin ~5 g → angiotensin II · for distributive shock with low SVR and high/normal SvO₂ · methylene blue is relatively contraindicated with serotonergic agents and in G6PD deficiency · resolved by confirming the vasoplegic phenotype (measured SVR) before escalating. [VAS]
