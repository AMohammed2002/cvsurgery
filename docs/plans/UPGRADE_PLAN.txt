================================================================================
CARDIACSCHOOL — DELIVERABLE-LEVEL OVERHAUL PLAN (v2)
From "14-day clerkship primer" to "the single resource that makes a student
think, talk, and operate-assist like a PGY5 cardiac surgery resident."
================================================================================

Author intent: this document replaces the old orchestration checklist. It is a
product/content specification, not a build script. It describes WHAT the finished
app should contain and WHY, in enough detail that any competent author (human or
agent) can produce the content without further clarification. No code is written
here. Every section is written to be handed to a content pod as a work order.

Reading guide:
  PART A — Where the app is today, and the gap to "PGY5."
  PART B — The content overhaul: what to add, day by day, to reach PGY5 depth.
  PART C — The Surgeries tab (the headline new deliverable) — full specification.
  PART D — Interactivity overhaul: turning reading into doing.
  PART E — Visual & experience overhaul.
  PART F — Assessment, spaced repetition, and the "PGY5 certification" layer.
  PART G — Exponential improvements (the ideas that change the ceiling, not the floor).
  PART H — Content authoring standards (so everything reads like one voice).
  PART I — Prioritized roadmap and acceptance criteria.

Throughout, "PGY5 level" is defined concretely (see A.3) so it is a testable bar,
not a slogan.

================================================================================
PART A — CURRENT STATE AND THE DEFINITION OF "PGY5"
================================================================================

A.1  WHAT EXISTS TODAY (honest inventory)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The app is a zero-build static site under site/. It ships:
  - A 14-day curriculum (curriculum.js): Anatomy, Coronary physiology, Valvular
    pathology, Imaging, Preop/risk, CABG, Valve surgery, Aortic/pericardial, MCS,
    CPB/cardioplegia, Hemostasis, Pacing, ICU/complications, and a Mock exam.
  - 43 micro-lessons broken into "chunks" (teaching prose + kboxes +
    embedded checkpoint MCQs).
  - ~285 flashcards and ~192 MCQs, lesson-gated (you must finish a lesson to
    unlock its cards and questions).
  - An Anatomy Review module (23 regions) plus a separate 124-card cloze anatomy
    deck (currently a second, parallel SRS engine).
  - A Trials module (17 landmark trials, 51 MCQs).
  - Interactive "exercises" (9 ordering drills + 5 label-the-diagram drills).
  - Gamification: XP, levels, day-streak, achievement badges.
  - A mock Royal College exam that draws 75 questions from the completed bank.

The teaching prose is genuinely good (see lessons-d1: mediastinal compartments,
pericardial compliance, chamber orientation, groove of Sondergaard). The problem
is not quality — it is COVERAGE, DEPTH CEILING, and MODE. The app teaches facts
and tests recall. It does not yet teach JUDGMENT, OPERATIVE THINKING, or
MANAGEMENT UNDER UNCERTAINTY — which is the difference between a strong clerk and
a senior resident.

A.2  THE STRUCTURAL GAPS (what a PGY5 has that this app does not teach)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. No operative fluency. A student can finish all 14 days and still not be able to
   narrate a single case start-to-finish: how the patient is positioned, where
   the lines go, the sequence from skin to cannulation to cross-clamp to weaning
   to closure, what the surgeon asks for at each step, and what goes wrong. This
   is the single biggest gap and the reason PART C (Surgeries tab) is the
   headline deliverable.
2. No "why this operation for this patient." The app teaches disease and teaches
   evidence separately. A PGY5 fuses them: given a specific patient, they argue
   the indication, choose the operation, choose the conduit/valve/cannulation
   strategy, and defend it against the Heart Team. The app needs decision content.
3. Shallow physiology. Coronary supply/demand and valve gradients are described in
   words, but the student never MANIPULATES a pressure-volume loop, a
   Frank-Starling curve, or a CPB oxygen-delivery equation. PGY5s reason in
   curves, not sentences.
4. No crisis management. Cardiac surgery is defined by low-frequency,
   high-lethality events: protamine reaction, malignant hyperthermia,
   post-bypass RV failure, massive air embolism, aortic dissection on cannulation,
   sudden VF, tamponade at 3 a.m. The app has none of these as rehearsable
   scenarios.
5. No ICU/postop reasoning engine. The ICU day lists complications; a PGY5 runs a
   low-cardiac-output algorithm at the bedside, titrates four drips, reads a
   Swan, and decides when to re-open the chest. This needs to be interactive.
6. No integration/synthesis assessment. The mock exam is 75 recall MCQs. Royal
   College / oral-board reality is the "long case" and rapid-fire viva. The app
   needs oral-exam-style, branched, defend-your-answer assessment.
7. No imaging fluency. "Imaging day" describes echo and cath in prose. A PGY5
   reads a TEE clip, a coronary angiogram, and a CT dissection in seconds. The
   app needs annotated image/loop libraries with read-out drills.

A.3  DEFINITION OF "PGY5-READY" (the testable bar every piece of content targets)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A user has reached the PGY5 bar for a topic when they can, WITHOUT PROMPTS:
  (a) State the indication for operation with the specific thresholds and the
      guideline class/level of evidence, AND name the trial(s) that set them.
  (b) Choose among competing operations/strategies and defend the choice for a
      SPECIFIC patient (age, comorbidity, anatomy), including when NOT to operate.
  (c) Narrate the operation step-by-step, name the instruments and sutures, state
      the numbers (ACT, flow index, cross-clamp limits, cardioplegia dosing), and
      predict the next step.
  (d) Anticipate the three most likely intra-op complications at each step and
      state the immediate management.
  (e) Run the first 24 postoperative hours: expected trajectory, the derangements
      to expect, the drips and their doses, and the triggers for escalation
      (re-exploration, MCS, dialysis).
  (f) Answer "why" three levels deep — mechanism, evidence, and exception.

Every lesson, flashcard, MCQ, surgery article, and simulation in this plan must
ladder up to at least one of (a)–(f). If a proposed piece of content does not, it
is trivia and should be cut.

================================================================================
PART B — CONTENT OVERHAUL: DAY-BY-DAY DEPTH TO PGY5
================================================================================

Principle: keep the 14-day spine (it is a good on-ramp) but add a second,
deeper LAYER on top of each day — "Attending Rounds" content — that is unlocked
after the day's core lessons. The core layer takes a novice to competent-clerk.
The Attending Rounds layer takes competent-clerk to PGY5. This preserves the
gentle on-ramp while raising the ceiling dramatically.

Each day below lists: (1) the depth gaps to fill in the core layer, and
(2) the new "Attending Rounds" advanced module to add. Target density per new
advanced module: 6–10 lessons-chunks, 8–12 flashcards, 6–10 MCQs, plus at least
one interactive element and one linked Surgery or Simulation.

B.1  DAY 1 — ANATOMY (core is strong; add surgical-anatomy depth)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps to fill:
  - Coronary dominance defined operationally (who supplies the PDA; why RCA-
    dominant matters for inferior MI and for grafting the PDA).
  - The interventricular and AV grooves as surgical landmarks; the course of the
    LAD in the anterior IV groove and its diagonal/septal branches; the danger of
    the "intramyocardial LAD."
  - The conduction axis in three dimensions: SA node at the SVC-RA junction (and
    why RA cannulation and Waterston/Sondergaard dissection threaten it), the AV
    node in the triangle of Koch (Todaro tendon, coronary sinus os, septal
    tricuspid leaflet), the His bundle penetrating at the membranous septum — the
    reason AVR and VSD closure cause heart block.
  - The aortic root as an anatomic unit: annulus, sinuses of Valsalva, coronary
    ostia, sinotubular junction, commissures; the fibrous continuity between the
    aortic and mitral valves (aortomitral curtain).
  - Venous/lymphatic drainage relevant to cannulation and to chylothorax.
Attending Rounds — "Surgical Anatomy of Danger":
  A module organized by "structures you can kill the patient with": the
  circumflex under the mitral annulus (P1 area), the AV node during tricuspid/
  septal work, the phrenic nerve on the pericardium and during LIMA harvest, the
  recurrent laryngeal around the arch/ligamentum, the thoracic duct, the
  membranous septum in AVR. Each with the operative maneuver that protects it.
  Ends with a linked "label the danger" interactive on a cross-sectional diagram.

B.2  DAY 2 — CORONARY PHYSIOLOGY (add the quantitative and the interventional)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps:
  - Myocardial oxygen extraction is near-maximal at rest (~70–80%), so increased
    demand MUST be met by increased flow — the mechanistic reason coronary
    stenosis causes angina. Make this a numeric, not a phrase.
  - Coronary flow is diastolic (LV), and the determinants of the diastolic
    perfusion gradient (aortic diastolic pressure minus LVEDP). This is the entire
    logic of the IABP and of why tachycardia is ischemic (shortened diastole).
  - Autoregulation, coronary flow reserve, and the concept of the critical
    stenosis (~70% diameter / ~90% area). Tie to FFR ≤ 0.80 and iFR.
  - Collateral circulation and coronary steal.
Attending Rounds — "Ischemia as a Decision":
  FFR/iFR-guided revascularization, the ISCHEMIA trial nuance (OMT vs early
  invasive in stable disease), viability assessment (hibernating vs stunned vs
  infarcted myocardium and why it changes the revascularization decision),
  and the supply/demand table used at the bedside to treat ischemia
  (lower demand: HR, wall tension, contractility; raise supply: FiO2, Hgb,
  diastolic pressure, relieve spasm). Interactive: a supply/demand "balance beam"
  the user tips by choosing interventions.

B.3  DAY 3 — VALVULAR PATHOLOGY (add staging, hemodynamics, and natural history)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps:
  - The four-stage ACC/AHA framework (A at-risk, B progressive, C asymptomatic
    severe [C1 compensated / C2 decompensated], D symptomatic severe) applied to
    AS, AR, MS, MR. This is the language of every valve decision.
  - Quantitative severity thresholds memorized cold: severe AS (Vmax ≥ 4 m/s,
    mean gradient ≥ 40 mmHg, AVA ≤ 1.0 cm2, dimensionless index), low-flow/low-
    gradient AS and the dobutamine challenge; severe MR (EROA, regurgitant volume,
    vena contracta); severe MS (MVA ≤ 1.5, then ≤ 1.0) and the pressure half-time.
  - Pressure-overload vs volume-overload remodeling and why the ventricle's
    response dictates the operative window (the "operate before the ventricle
    fails" principle). LVESD and EF triggers for asymptomatic severe AR/MR.
  - Primary vs secondary MR — a genuinely different disease with a different
    operation and different evidence (COAPT vs MITRA-FR).
Attending Rounds — "The Valve Clinic":
  Case-based: for each of six patients, walk the stage → symptom → imaging → Heart
  Team decision → repair-vs-replace → mechanical-vs-tissue → surgical-vs-
  transcatheter. Pull in the age/anticoagulation tradeoff explicitly. Interactive:
  a "when do you operate?" slider timeline where the user places the operative
  moment on the natural-history curve and sees survival consequences.

B.4  DAY 4 — IMAGING (turn description into READING)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps:
  - The standard TEE exam: the mid-esophageal views (4-chamber, 2-chamber, LAX,
    aortic valve SAX/LAX, bicaval), transgastric views for gradients, and what
    each is FOR. The intraoperative TEE checklist before and after CPB.
  - Reading a coronary angiogram: naming segments, describing a lesion (location,
    %stenosis, length, calcification, TIMI flow), and the standard views that open
    up the LAD/circ/RCA.
  - The dissection CT: true vs false lumen signs, entry tear, malperfusion,
    Stanford A vs B, and the measurements that drive operation.
  - Echo of the failing post-op heart: tamponade physiology, RV assessment (TAPSE,
    S'), volume status, LVOT obstruction/SAM after mitral repair.
Attending Rounds — "Read-Out Drills":
  This day is transformed by the imaging library in PART D.4. The advanced module
  is a set of annotated loops/stills with hotspot questions ("point to the entry
  tear," "grade this MR," "which segment is occluded"). This is the single
  highest-yield interactivity upgrade for imaging.

B.5  DAY 5 — PREOP & RISK (make risk quantitative and actionable)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps:
  - STS-PROM vs EuroSCORE II: what each includes, calibration, and the honest
    limits (neither captures frailty, porcelain aorta, hostile chest well).
  - Frailty, the "eyeball test," gait speed, and how they move a borderline
    patient from surgical to transcatheter.
  - Preoperative optimization that changes outcomes: anemia/iron, glycemic
    control, smoking, prehabilitation, timing after MI/stent (DAPT interruption
    logistics), carotid disease, dental clearance for valve surgery.
  - The workup that must be complete before pump: coronaries, carotids, PFTs when
    indicated, dentition, CT for redo/aortic, group-and-crossmatch, echo.
Attending Rounds — "Building the Case for Operation":
  A structured Heart Team simulation: the user is dealt a patient dossier and must
  assemble the indication, quantify risk two ways, list the optimization steps,
  and write the one-paragraph "why we are operating and why now." Interactive:
  a risk calculator that lets the user toggle comorbidities and watch STS move.

B.6  DAY 6 — CABG (add conduit science, completeness, and the evidence in depth)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps:
  - Conduit biology and patency: LIMA-to-LAD (>90% at 10y and WHY — the internal
    mammary's endothelial/vasodilator biology and resistance to atherosclerosis),
    radial vs saphenous vein (RADIAL trials), the total-arterial and BIMA debate
    (ART trial, sternal wound risk in diabetics), no-touch vein harvest.
  - Completeness of revascularization and the concept of grafting all vessels
    > 1.5 mm supplying viable myocardium.
  - On-pump vs off-pump (ROOBY, CORONARY): the real tradeoffs and who benefits.
  - The evidence architecture: SYNTAX score and the anatomy → decision map,
    FREEDOM (diabetics → CABG), EXCEL/NOBLE (left main) and the still-live
    controversy. This day should make the student able to argue CABG vs PCI for a
    named patient using the exact trials.
Attending Rounds — "Designing the Operation":
  Given a coronary map, the user plans the grafts (which conduit to which target,
  sequential vs individual, proximal anastomosis strategy, off- vs on-pump). Links
  directly to the CABG surgery article in PART C.

B.7  DAY 7 — VALVE SURGERY (repair-vs-replace and prosthesis choice in depth)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps:
  - Aortic: SAVR vs TAVR by risk and age (PARTNER 1/2/3, Evolut), valve-in-valve,
    the durability-vs-invasiveness curve, bicuspid and small-annulus problems,
    the Ross and its niche.
  - Mitral repair science: degenerative (Carpentier functional classification,
    leaflet resection vs neochords, annuloplasty ring sizing, the "repair is
    better than replace" survival data) and the systolic-anterior-motion trap.
  - Mechanical vs tissue: the age thresholds, the lifelong-anticoagulation
    tradeoff, structural valve deterioration, and shared decision-making.
  - Tricuspid: the under-treated valve, when to add a ring at left-sided surgery.
Attending Rounds — "Repair or Replace, and With What":
  Six valve patients, each forcing one hard call. Interactive decision tree with
  the evidence surfaced at each branch. Links to SAVR and mitral-repair surgery
  articles.

B.8  DAY 8 — AORTIC & PERICARDIAL (the emergencies; add operative strategy)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps:
  - Aneurysm thresholds (5.5 cm ascending, lower for bicuspid/Marfan/family
    history/rapid growth), the root-vs-supracoronary decision, valve-sparing root
    (David/Yacoub).
  - Type A dissection as a surgical emergency: the clock, malperfusion syndromes,
    cannulation strategy (axillary/femoral/central), circulatory arrest with
    antegrade cerebral perfusion, the hemiarch vs total arch decision, the frozen
    elephant trunk.
  - Type B: medical management, TEVAR indications, complicated vs uncomplicated.
  - Pericardial disease: tamponade physiology (pulsus paradoxus, the equalized
    diastolic pressures, echo signs), constrictive vs restrictive, the window
    vs pericardiectomy decision.
Attending Rounds — "The 2 a.m. Dissection":
  A time-pressured branched simulation from ED call to sternotomy: recognize,
  image, resuscitate to permissive parameters, choose cannulation, choose arch
  strategy. This is also a flagship crisis simulation (PART D.3).

B.9  DAY 9 — MECHANICAL CIRCULATORY SUPPORT (add the physiology and the decisions)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps:
  - IABP: exact mechanism (diastolic augmentation raises coronary perfusion,
    systolic deflation reduces afterload), timing errors and their waveforms,
    contraindications (AR, dissection), the honest evidence (IABP-SHOCK II).
  - ECMO: VA vs VV, the physiology of VA-ECMO (retrograde aortic flow, LV
    distension and the need to vent, north-south/Harlequin syndrome,
    differential hypoxia), cannulation, weaning, complications.
  - Durable LVAD: continuous-flow physiology, INTERMACS profiles, the destination-
    vs-bridge decision, driveline infection, GI bleeding/AVMs, pump thrombosis,
    RV failure after LVAD.
  - Transplant: allocation, the donor heart, primary graft dysfunction, rejection
    and immunosuppression basics.
Attending Rounds — "Escalating Support":
  A ladder simulation: a failing heart the user must support one rung at a time
  (inotropes → IABP → temporary VAD/ECMO → durable device/transplant), choosing
  the right device for the failure mode (LV vs RV vs biV vs respiratory).

B.10 DAY 10 — CPB & CARDIOPLEGIA (already deep; add troubleshooting and the "feel")
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps:
  - The circuit as a system the student can draw from memory: venous reservoir,
    pump (roller vs centrifugal), oxygenator, heat exchanger, arterial filter,
    cardioplegia delivery, vents and suckers, the safety devices (level/bubble
    sensors).
  - The numbers cold: ACT > 400–480 for bypass, flow index 2.2–2.5 L/min/m2,
    MAP targets, hematocrit on pump, temperature strategies, alpha- vs pH-stat and
    WHEN each is used.
  - Myocardial protection in depth: antegrade vs retrograde, del Nido vs blood
    cardioplegia dosing intervals, the reason for topical cooling and for the
    "hot shot" reperfusion dose.
  - Troubleshooting: high line pressure, low venous return, rising reservoir,
    the differential for "won't come off pump."
Attending Rounds — "On the Pump":
  A perfusion simulation (see PART D.2 physiology labs): the user sets flow,
  temperature, and cardioplegia and manages events (venous air lock, sudden
  hypotension on initiation of bypass = dissection until proven otherwise).

B.11 DAY 11 — HEMOSTASIS (add the algorithm and the products)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps:
  - Heparin/protamine dosing and the protamine reactions (three types, including
    catastrophic pulmonary vasoconstriction), heparin resistance and ATIII.
  - The post-bypass coagulopathy: dilution, platelet dysfunction from the circuit,
    hypothermia, hyperfibrinolysis, residual heparin — and how to tell them apart
    with TEG/ROTEM and a targeted product strategy.
  - The surgical-vs-medical bleeding decision and the criteria for re-exploration
    (rate-based chest-tube thresholds, the "sudden stop then tamponade" trap).
  - Antifibrinolytics (tranexamic acid), factor concentrates, cell salvage,
    Jehovah's Witness bloodless strategies.
Attending Rounds — "The Bleeding Chest":
  A branched scenario: chest tubes at 300 mL/hr — is it surgical or coagulopathic?
  The user orders TEG, interprets it, gives targeted product, and decides on
  re-exploration. Interactive TEG interpretation drill.

B.12 DAY 12 — PACING & CONDUCTION (add wire management and the rhythm algorithms)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps:
  - Epicardial wires: atrial vs ventricular, how to test capture and sensing,
    the pacing modes (AAI/VVI/DDD) and when each is used post-op.
  - Post-op AF: incidence, prevention (beta-blockade, amiodarone, the debate),
    rate vs rhythm, anticoagulation decision and stroke risk.
  - Heart block after AVR/mitral/septal work: the anatomy (B.1), temporary pacing,
    and the threshold to permanent pacemaker.
  - The unstable rhythms in the unit: VT/VF, the pause-dependent situations,
    pacing to overdrive.
Attending Rounds — "Rhythm at the Bedside":
  Interactive: a pacemaker box the user must program to the clinical situation
  (failure to capture → raise output; oversensing → adjust sensitivity;
  complete heart block → set a backup rate). Rhythm-strip read-out drills.

B.13 DAY 13 — CARDIAC ICU & COMPLICATIONS (turn the list into an ENGINE)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core gaps:
  - Low cardiac output syndrome as an ALGORITHM: define it (CI < 2.0–2.2, rising
    lactate, oliguria, cool periphery), then the systematic work-up
    (preload → afterload → contractility → rate/rhythm → tamponade → ischemia →
    mechanical), and the ordered response.
  - The Swan-Ganz numbers and the four hemodynamic profiles; when a Swan changes
    management and when it does not.
  - Vasoplegia (the post-CPB distributive shock): recognition and the escalation
    ladder (norepinephrine → vasopressin → methylene blue → hydroxocobalamin →
    angiotensin II).
  - RV failure, tamponade (including the low-pressure/localized clot after cardiac
    surgery that echo can miss), the differential for the hypotensive post-op
    patient, and the trigger to re-open at the bedside.
  - The other-organ complications: AKI/CRRT, stroke/delirium, respiratory failure,
    sternal wound infection/mediastinitis, GI (mesenteric ischemia), the systemic
    inflammatory response.
Attending Rounds — "Running the Unit":
  The flagship ICU simulation (PART D.3): a fresh post-op patient whose numbers
  drift; the user runs the LCOS algorithm, titrates drips, calls for a Swan,
  echoes for tamponade, and decides on re-exploration or MCS. This is the capstone
  that ties Days 9, 10, 12, and 13 together.

B.14 DAY 14 — replaced by the assessment overhaul (PART F). The single 75-MCQ
     exam becomes a multi-format "certification" including oral-style vivas and a
     long case.

================================================================================
PART C — THE SURGERIES TAB (HEADLINE NEW DELIVERABLE)
================================================================================

This is the centerpiece the user asked for: a dedicated "Surgeries" section where
each operation has a long-form article (minimum 500 words, target 1,200–2,500 for
the flagship operations) that takes a reader who knows NOTHING about the operation
or the anatomy and leaves them thinking at a PGY5 level — plus its own flashcards
and MCQs. It should feel like the best operative chapter a resident ever read,
made interactive.

C.1  INFORMATION ARCHITECTURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
New top-level nav item: "Surgeries" (scalpel icon), route #/surgery.
Landing page: a grid of operation cards grouped by category:
  - Coronary: CABG (on-pump), OPCAB (off-pump), redo CABG.
  - Aortic valve: SAVR, TAVR/TAVI, Ross procedure, aortic root replacement
    (Bentall), valve-sparing root (David).
  - Mitral valve: mitral repair (degenerative), mitral replacement,
    transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER/MitraClip).
  - Other valve: tricuspid repair, double-valve.
  - Aorta: ascending/hemiarch replacement, total arch + frozen elephant trunk,
    type A dissection repair, TEVAR.
  - MCS/transplant: IABP insertion, ECMO cannulation (peripheral VA), durable
    LVAD implant, heart transplant.
  - Foundational operative skills (their own short articles): median sternotomy,
    going on and coming off cardiopulmonary bypass, aortic and venous cannulation,
    cardioplegia delivery, de-airing, sternal closure, redo sternotomy,
    pericardiocentesis, IABP, chest re-exploration for bleeding/tamponade.
Each card shows: title, category, estimated read time, difficulty, and a
completion/mastery ring.

C.2  THE ARTICLE TEMPLATE (every surgery article follows this exact spine)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Each article is structured so a novice can start at the top and a PGY5 can jump to
the middle. Sections, in order:

  1. ONE-LINE ORIENTATION — what this operation is and what problem it solves,
     in a single plain sentence a first-year student understands.
  2. THE PROBLEM (indications & who gets it) — the disease in brief, the exact
     indication thresholds, the guideline class/level, the competing options, and
     the "why this operation for this patient." Names the landmark trials and
     links to the Trials module.
  3. RELEVANT ANATOMY (assume zero knowledge) — a from-scratch tour of exactly the
     anatomy this operation touches, with the diagram and the danger structures.
     This is what lets a novice start cold. Links to the Anatomy Review module.
  4. PREOPERATIVE CHECKLIST — workup that must be complete, consent points, the
     specific imaging the surgeon needs in the room, positioning, lines/monitors,
     anesthetic considerations, antibiotics/timing.
  5. THE OPERATION, STEP BY STEP — the heart of the article. A numbered sequence
     from skin to closure. For EACH step: what is done, what instruments/sutures
     are used, what the surgeon is thinking, the specific numbers/targets, and the
     "what the student does" (retract here, load this suture, watch this). Written
     so the reader can narrate the case afterward. This section alone should be
     300–1,000+ words for flagship operations.
  6. THE NUMBERS — a quick-reference block of every quantitative target in the
     case (ACT, flow index, cross-clamp time expectations, cardioplegia dosing
     intervals, sizing, pressures).
  7. WHAT GOES WRONG (complications by step) — the three-to-six most important
     intra-op and early post-op complications, each mapped to the step where it
     arises, its recognition, and its immediate management. This is where PGY5
     judgment lives.
  8. THE FIRST 24 HOURS — expected postoperative trajectory and the specific
     things to watch for THIS operation (e.g., heart block after AVR, RV failure
     after LVAD, malperfusion after dissection repair, SAM after mitral repair).
  9. EVIDENCE & CONTROVERSY — the trials and the still-live debates
     (BIMA, on/off-pump, TAVR-vs-SAVR by age, repair-vs-replace).
 10. HOW TO SOUND LIKE A PGY5 — a short "viva prep" block: the five questions an
     examiner will ask about this operation and the crisp answers. Directly
     ladders to the A.3 bar.
 11. LINKED PRACTICE — the article's own flashcards and MCQs (C.4), plus links to
     the matching interactive walkthrough (D.1) and any simulation.

Minimum 500 words is the floor for the smallest skill articles; flagship
operations (CABG, SAVR, mitral repair, type A dissection, LVAD, going on/off
bypass) should run 1,500–2,500 words because the step-by-step and complications
sections carry the PGY5 payload.

C.3  WORKED EXAMPLE — CABG ARTICLE OUTLINE (so authors see the target)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
To make the template concrete, here is the intended shape and depth of the CABG
article (author fills the prose to the standards in PART H):

  1. Orientation: "A coronary artery bypass graft reroutes blood around a blocked
     coronary artery using a spare vessel, restoring flow to starved heart muscle."
  2. The problem: stable multivessel/left-main disease, the ischemic burden, the
     SYNTAX/FREEDOM/EXCEL evidence, CABG-vs-PCI for the specific patient
     (diabetic, low EF, complex anatomy), and when medical therapy wins (ISCHEMIA
     nuance). Class I indications stated with thresholds.
  3. Anatomy from scratch: the coronary tree (LM → LAD with diagonals/septals; LCx
     with OMs; RCA → PDA/PLB), dominance, the grooves the vessels run in, the
     LIMA behind the chest wall, the saphenous vein and radial artery as conduits,
     and the danger structures (phrenic on the LIMA pedicle).
  4. Preop: viability/coronary map in the room, Allen's test for radial, vein
     mapping, DAPT timing, antibiotics, positioning, arterial line/Swan, TEE.
  5. Step by step: sternotomy → harvest LIMA (skeletonized vs pedicled) and vein/
     radial simultaneously → heparinize to ACT > 400 → aortic + venous (two-stage)
     cannulation → on bypass → cross-clamp + antegrade cardioplegia to arrest →
     identify targets, arteriotomy → distal anastomoses (LIMA-to-LAD last or per
     strategy; 7-0/8-0 Prolene, parachute technique) → de-air, remove clamp →
     proximal anastomoses on partial occlusion clamp (or during a single clamp
     strategy) → wean from bypass → protamine → hemostasis → wires + drains →
     sternal closure. Instruments and sutures named at each step; targets stated.
  6. The numbers: ACT > 400–480; flow index 2.2–2.5; cardioplegia dosing; graft
     flow measurement (transit-time) acceptable values.
  7. What goes wrong: incomplete revascularization, graft kinking/tension,
     LIMA injury/spasm, aortic dissection off the proximal clamp/cannula, RV
     ischemia from air down the RCA graft, sternal wound (esp. BIMA + diabetes),
     early graft occlusion presenting as post-op ischemia.
  8. First 24 h: expected course, the ischemic ECG/enzyme trap, bleeding, AF,
     the low-output patient.
  9. Evidence & controversy: LIMA-to-LAD dogma, total-arterial/BIMA, off-pump,
     hybrid revascularization.
 10. PGY5 viva: "Why LIMA to LAD?" "When PCI over CABG?" "Off-pump — who
     benefits?" "How do you confirm graft patency in the OR?" "Manage new anterior
     ST changes 2 hours post-op."
 11. Practice: 10 flashcards + 8 MCQs + the interactive CABG walkthrough.

Every operation in C.1 gets an article at this level of specificity. Prioritize
the flagships (C.6) first.

C.7  WORKED EXAMPLE — SAVR ARTICLE OUTLINE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  1. Orientation: "A surgical aortic valve replacement removes a diseased aortic
     valve and sews in a new one, relieving the obstruction that is strangling the
     left ventricle."
  2. The problem: severe symptomatic AS (Stage D) and asymptomatic severe AS with
     LV dysfunction, an abnormal exercise test, or very severe gradients; the
     TAVR-vs-SAVR decision by age and surgical risk (PARTNER 3 and Evolut Low-Risk
     for younger/low-risk toward TAVR debate, durability and lifetime-management
     arguments for SAVR in the young); mechanical-vs-tissue by age (~60 threshold)
     and anticoagulation willingness; the small-annulus and bicuspid problems.
  3. Anatomy from scratch: the aortic root as a unit — annulus, three sinuses of
     Valsalva, the right and left coronary ostia sitting in their sinuses, the
     sinotubular junction, the three commissures, and the fibrous aortomitral
     curtain; the conduction axis running just beneath the non/right commissure
     (the reason AVR causes heart block); the relationship of the right coronary
     ostium to the annulus (the reason a low right-coronary button is a hazard in
     root work).
  4. Preop: gradient/AVA/dimensionless index confirmed, coronary angiogram (graft
     concomitant disease), dental clearance (endocarditis prevention), annular
     sizing on CT/echo, choice of prosthesis discussed with the patient,
     group-and-crossmatch, arterial line/Swan/TEE.
  5. Step by step: median sternotomy → pericardial cradle → heparinize (ACT > 400)
     → aortic and two-stage venous cannulation, plus a retrograde cardioplegia
     catheter in the coronary sinus and an LV vent → on bypass → cross-clamp →
     antegrade cardioplegia to arrest (switch to direct ostial or retrograde once
     the aorta is open because antegrade will leak through the incompetent valve)
     → transverse or hockey-stick aortotomy above the STJ → excise the diseased
     leaflets and debride annular calcium meticulously (do not perforate the
     annulus or embolize calcium) → size the annulus → place pledgeted
     interrupted mattress sutures around the annulus (supra- vs intra-annular) →
     seat and tie the prosthesis → close the aortotomy → de-air thoroughly (air
     in the aortic root goes down the right coronary and stuns the RV) → remove
     cross-clamp → wean from bypass, confirm seating and no paravalvular leak on
     TEE → protamine, hemostasis, wires (heart-block risk!), drains, close.
  6. The numbers: ACT > 400–480; cross-clamp time expectations for isolated SAVR;
     antegrade/retrograde cardioplegia dosing intervals; prosthesis sizes and the
     effective orifice area / patient-prosthesis mismatch threshold (indexed EOA).
  7. What goes wrong: complete heart block (the conduction axis under the
     commissure) needing pacing/PPM; paravalvular leak (poor annular sutures or
     debridement); coronary ostial obstruction (high-seated valve or a low
     button); annular rupture/AV disruption in a heavily calcified root; patient-
     prosthesis mismatch in a small annulus (the argument for a root enlargement);
     air embolism to the RCA causing post-clamp RV dysfunction; stroke from
     calcium embolization.
  8. First 24 h: watch the rhythm (transient and permanent block), the gradient/
     leak on TEE, bleeding, and LV recovery in the previously pressure-overloaded
     ventricle (diastolic dysfunction, preload dependence).
  9. Evidence & controversy: TAVR-vs-SAVR by age and risk and the lifetime-
     management framing; mechanical-vs-tissue and the shrinking age threshold;
     sutureless/rapid-deployment valves; root enlargement vs mismatch.
 10. PGY5 viva: "Why does AVR cause heart block?" "Sizing — how do you avoid
     patient-prosthesis mismatch?" "New complete heart block post-op — your plan?"
     "TAVR or SAVR for a fit 68-year-old?" "Why de-air obsessively before the
     clamp comes off?"
 11. Practice: 10 flashcards + 8 MCQs + the SAVR walkthrough.

C.8  WORKED EXAMPLE — TYPE A AORTIC DISSECTION REPAIR (a flagship emergency)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  1. Orientation: "An acute type A dissection is a tear in the wall of the
     ascending aorta that is killing the patient by the hour; the operation
     replaces the torn segment before it ruptures or shuts off blood to the brain,
     heart, or body."
  2. The problem: the ~1–2%/hour mortality of untreated type A, the Stanford A
     (any ascending involvement = surgical) vs B distinction, malperfusion
     syndromes (cerebral, coronary, mesenteric, renal, limb) and how they change
     the plan, and the contraindication landscape (the operation is almost always
     indicated because the alternative is death).
  3. Anatomy from scratch: the three-layered aortic wall and what a "true vs false
     lumen" means; the ascending aorta, arch and its head vessels (innominate,
     left carotid, left subclavian), the aortic root and coronary ostia (a
     dissection flap can occlude the RCA and cause inferior MI), and the
     brachiocephalic anatomy that drives cannulation choice.
  4. Preop: this is a resuscitation-and-go — gated CT angiogram of the whole aorta,
     blood pressure and pain control to permissive parameters, massive-transfusion
     readiness, TEE, and rapid consent; the clock is the enemy.
  5. Step by step: arterial cannulation chosen to give antegrade true-lumen flow
     (right axillary/subclavian, or central/femoral with awareness of retrograde
     false-lumen risk) → venous cannulation, cool the patient → on bypass, cool to
     the target for circulatory arrest → cross-clamp if feasible, arrest the heart
     → open the aorta, identify the primary entry tear → deep hypothermic (or
     moderate + antegrade cerebral perfusion via the axillary/innominate)
     circulatory arrest to inspect and repair the arch → resect the torn segment,
     obliterate the false lumen (glue/felt/sandwich), and reconstruct: a
     supracoronary graft for a limited tear, a hemiarch for arch involvement, a
     full root replacement (Bentall) or valve-sparing root if the root/valve is
     involved, and a frozen elephant trunk for extensive arch/descending disease →
     re-establish antegrade perfusion, rewarm → de-air, wean, meticulous hemostasis
     (dissected tissue bleeds everywhere) → close.
  6. The numbers: the temperature targets for circulatory arrest strategies, safe
     antegrade-cerebral-perfusion flows, the arrest-time thresholds that drive the
     hypothermia depth, ACT on bypass.
  7. What goes wrong: malperfusion that persists after repair, coronary
     involvement needing a graft, catastrophic coagulopathic bleeding, stroke from
     the arch work or cannulation strategy, spinal cord injury with extensive
     descending work, and the reperfusion physiology of a cooled, arrested patient.
  8. First 24 h: neurologic exam is king (did we protect the brain?), bleeding and
     the coagulopathy, malperfusion reassessment, blood-pressure control to
     protect the repair and any residual downstream dissection.
  9. Evidence & controversy: cannulation-site debate (axillary vs central vs
     femoral), DHCA vs moderate hypothermia + ACP, hemiarch vs total arch vs FET,
     and how aggressive to be on the root.
 10. PGY5 viva: "Type A on the CT — what are the first three things you do?"
     "Why does cannulation site matter here?" "How do you protect the brain during
     arch repair?" "Inferior ST changes with the dissection — what's happening?"
     "Hemiarch or total arch — how do you decide?"
 11. Practice: 8 flashcards + 8 MCQs + the 2 a.m.-dissection crisis simulation.

C.9  WORKED EXAMPLE — FOUNDATIONAL SKILL: GOING ON AND OFF BYPASS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is a "skill" article (shorter than a flagship operation but still full-depth)
and it is the connective tissue of every pump case, so it is Tier 1.
  1. Orientation: "Going on bypass hands the job of the heart and lungs to a
     machine so the surgeon can stop and open the heart; coming off gives the job
     back — and both handoffs are where patients are lost."
  2. The problem/why: every open-heart operation lives inside this maneuver; doing
     it smoothly and recognizing when it is going wrong is a core PGY-competency.
  3. Anatomy from scratch: the ascending aorta (arterial inflow), the right atrium
     and cavae (venous drainage), the coronary sinus (retrograde cardioplegia), and
     the LV/pulmonary veins (venting) — i.e., every cannulation site and why.
  4. Preop/setup: heparinize and confirm ACT > 400 BEFORE any cannula goes in,
     purse-string sutures, the sequence of aortic-then-venous cannulation, and
     de-airing the lines.
  5. Step by step — GOING ON: confirm ACT → cannulate aorta (test with a small
     flow — a flat/blunted trace or a discolored aorta means dissection, STOP) →
     cannulate the venous cavae/RA → connect and de-air → perfusionist initiates
     forward flow, drains venous return, the heart empties → verify the MAP,
     venous return, and that the face/pupils/oxygenator look right → cross-clamp,
     deliver cardioplegia, arrest → operate.
     Step by step — COMING OFF: rewarm to normothermia → de-air the heart
     thoroughly (TEE-guided) → remove cross-clamp, let the heart reperfuse and
     recover a rhythm (pace or defibrillate as needed) → resume ventilation →
     gradually reduce pump flow while the perfusionist fills the heart, watching
     the arterial trace and TEE as the ventricle takes over → wean fully, assess
     the hemodynamics and the repair on TEE → give protamine slowly (watch for
     reaction) → decannulate → hemostasis.
  6. The numbers: ACT > 400–480; flow index 2.2–2.5 L/min/m2; MAP targets;
     the "empty and beating vs arrested" states; protamine dosing to reverse.
  7. What goes wrong: aortic dissection on cannulation (the flat trace); venous
     air lock / poor drainage (rising reservoir, low return); the "won't come off
     pump" differential (residual ischemia/incomplete revascularization, valve
     problem, RV failure, arrhythmia, air down the RCA, tamponade physiology,
     protamine reaction, vasoplegia); protamine reactions (three types).
  8. First minutes/hours: the newly-weaned heart is fragile — preload, rhythm,
     and afterload all matter; low output here triggers the LCOS algorithm (B.13).
  9. Evidence & controversy: temperature and de-airing strategies; centrifugal vs
     roller pumps; minimally invasive cannulation.
 10. PGY5 viva: "You start bypass and the arterial line pressure goes flat — what
     is it until proven otherwise, and what do you do?" "Walk me through weaning."
     "The heart won't come off pump — give me your differential." "Why give
     protamine slowly?"
 11. Practice: 8 flashcards + 8 MCQs + the going-on-bypass walkthrough and the
     dissection-on-cannulation crisis sim.

C.10 SAMPLE PRACTICE ITEMS (the register the surgery flashcards/MCQs must hit)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
To calibrate authors, here are model items for the CABG article (author produces
8–12 cards and 6–10 MCQs per article at this level):
  Flashcard (front): "Why is the LIMA-to-LAD graft the cornerstone of every CABG?"
  Flashcard (back): "The internal mammary is uniquely resistant to atherosclerosis
     (its endothelium produces nitric oxide and it has a non-fenestrated internal
     elastic lamina), so LIMA-to-LAD patency exceeds ~90% at 10 years and drives
     the survival benefit of surgery — which is why it is protected and placed to
     the most important vessel, the LAD."
  MCQ (judgment level): "You are 2 hours out from an on-pump CABG (LIMA-LAD, SVG
     to OM and PDA). The monitor shows new ST elevation in II, III, aVF and the
     patient becomes hypotensive. What is the most likely mechanism and the next
     best step?" Options include air/kinking of the RCA-territory graft (correct
     mechanism), and the next step (echo/return to OR / graft interrogation) vs
     distractors (give fluid alone, uptitrate pressor alone, thrombolyse). The
     explanation teaches why an early territory-specific change points to a graft
     problem and why the answer is to interrogate/revise, not to medicate around it.
  MCQ (step/sequence level): "During an on-pump CABG, which must be true BEFORE the
     aortic cannula is placed?" Answer: ACT confirmed > 400 (heparinized). The
     explanation teaches the catastrophe of cannulating an unheparinized aorta.
These show the target: cards and questions that test JUDGMENT, SEQUENCE, NUMBERS,
and COMPLICATIONS — not just "what is the indication."

PART D — INTERACTIVITY OVERHAUL: FROM READING TO DOING
================================================================================

The app currently has two interactive modes (ordering + label-the-diagram). PGY5
thinking is decision-making under constraints, so the new interactivity is built
around DECISIONS and MANIPULATION, not recall.

D.1  SURGERY WALKTHROUGHS (branched operative decision trees)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
For each Tier-1/2 operation, a branched, step-by-step "you are operating"
walkthrough, separate from but linked to the article:
  - At each step the user chooses the next move from realistic options (e.g.,
    "the aortic pressure trace goes flat as you start bypass — your move?" →
    options include "call dissection, stop, re-cannulate" [correct] vs "increase
    flow" [worsens]).
  - Wrong choices branch to a consequence and a teaching recovery, not just "X."
  - Correct paths advance; the walkthrough tracks a "smoothness" score.
  - Completion awards XP (reuse addXP/streak) and contributes to operation mastery.
Isolate this as its own module (js/surgery/, css/surgery.css, window.Surgery,
route #/surgery/<op>/walkthrough) with its own validator, mirroring how the
Trials and Anatomy modules are already isolated so app.js risk is contained.

D.2  PHYSIOLOGY LABS (live, slider-driven curves)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The highest-leverage way to teach PGY5 physiology is to let the student MOVE the
curve. Build a Physiology Labs module (js/physiology/, window.Physio,
route #/physiology) with these live simulations:
  - LV pressure-volume loop: sliders for preload, afterload, and contractility;
    presets for AS, AR, MR, MS, tamponade, and a normal loop; the loop redraws
    live and reports EF, stroke work, and EDP. The single best tool for valve and
    heart-failure physiology.
  - Frank-Starling / ventricular function family of curves, with the effect of
    inotropes and failure shown as curve shifts.
  - Coronary supply/demand balance beam (ties to B.2): interventions tip the beam.
  - CPB oxygen delivery: sliders for flow index, hematocrit, and temperature that
    compute DO2 and show the safe/unsafe zone (ties to B.10).
  - IABP timing: a drag-the-inflation-point trace where early/late inflation and
    deflation produce the classic waveform errors.
  - Valve gradient/orifice relationship (continuity equation made visual).
Each lab has a short "what am I looking at" preface, 3–5 guided tasks
("make this loop look like aortic stenosis"), and awards XP on task completion.

Fully-specified example lab — "THE PRESSURE-VOLUME LOOP" (author target for depth):
  - Canvas: a live LV pressure (y) vs volume (x) loop with the ESPVR (end-systolic
    pressure-volume relationship, the contractility line) and the EDPVR (the
    passive filling curve) drawn as the bounding rails; the four phases labeled
    (filling, isovolumic contraction, ejection, isovolumic relaxation).
  - Sliders: PRELOAD (shifts the filling volume / loop width), AFTERLOAD (the
    arterial elastance line the loop ejects against), and CONTRACTILITY (rotates
    the ESPVR slope). Each slider redraws the loop in real time and updates a
    readout: EDV, ESV, stroke volume, ejection fraction, and stroke work (the loop
    area).
  - Presets that snap the loop to a disease so the student SEES the pathology:
      * Aortic stenosis: tall, narrow loop with a high systolic pressure (the LV
        generates huge pressure to eject across the fixed obstruction) and
        concentric hypertrophy physiology.
      * Aortic regurgitation: wide loop shifted right, no true isovolumic phases
        (the leaky valve means filling and ejection blur), large stroke volume,
        eccentric/volume overload.
      * Mitral regurgitation: the loop loses its isovolumic contraction phase (the
        LV unloads backward into the LA the instant pressure rises), high total
        stroke volume masking forward failure.
      * Mitral stenosis: an under-filled loop (the LV is preload-starved behind the
        tight valve), small stroke volume, with the disease living upstream.
      * Tamponade: a small, cramped loop from impaired filling.
  - Guided tasks (each awards XP): "Drag the sliders until this loop shows aortic
    stenosis, then explain in one line why the systolic pressure is so high."
    "Show what an acute afterload increase does to stroke volume." "Load the AR
    preset and explain why there is no true isovolumic relaxation phase." Each task
    reveals a model explanation for self-check and links to the matching Day-3
    valve lesson.
  - Why it is the flagship lab: valve disease, heart failure, and the entire logic
    of afterload reduction and inotropes become visual and manipulable. A student
    who can drive this loop to each preset and narrate why has crossed from
    "memorized the murmur" to "understands the ventricle" — the A.3(f) bar.

D.3  CRISIS SIMULATIONS (the code-scenario engine)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A timed, branched scenario engine for the low-frequency/high-lethality events:
  - Post-bypass RV failure; protamine reaction; malignant hyperthermia; massive
    air embolism; aortic dissection on cannulation; sudden VF; the bleeding chest
    (ties to B.11); tamponade at the bedside; vasoplegia refractory to pressors;
    the 2 a.m. type A dissection (B.8).
  - Each scenario: an evolving vitals panel, a decision every N seconds, branching
    consequences, and an after-action debrief that scores recognition speed and
    correctness and links back to the relevant lesson/article.
Reuses the walkthrough engine with a clock. This is what converts "I read about
vasoplegia" into "I have managed vasoplegia ten times."

Fully-specified example scenario — "PROTAMINE REACTION" (so authors see the depth):
  - Setup: isolated SAVR, weaning complete, TEE looks good, perfusionist begins
    protamine. Vitals panel starts normal.
  - Beat 1 (t+15s): systemic pressure falls, PA pressure rises sharply, the heart
    distends on TEE, the field goes tense. Decision: "What is happening?" Options —
    (a) type II/III protamine reaction with pulmonary vasoconstriction [correct],
    (b) LV failure, (c) tamponade, (d) inadequate reversal. Wrong picks branch to a
    consequence and a teaching nudge back.
  - Beat 2: "Immediate actions?" Multi-select — STOP the protamine, call it out to
    the team, support the circulation (fluids/pressor, consider re-heparinize and
    go back on pump), treat the pulmonary hypertension, be ready to re-establish
    bypass. Half-measures (e.g., just push more pressor) worsen the trace.
  - Beat 3: "The RV is failing from the acute pulmonary hypertension — next?"
    Options include going back on bypass to rest the heart, inhaled pulmonary
    vasodilator, inotropic RV support; the sim rewards recognizing that reloading
    the failing RV against high PVR is the danger.
  - Debrief: scores recognition speed and the correctness of the ordered actions,
    names the three protamine-reaction types, and links to the Day-11 hemostasis
    lesson and the going-off-bypass article. Each crisis scenario is authored to
    this level: 3–6 branching beats, an evolving vitals panel, consequence
    branches for wrong turns, and a debrief that links back to the source content
    and feeds the mastery model.

Minimum crisis library to ship in Phase 4 (three of these): protamine reaction,
aortic dissection on cannulation, post-bypass RV failure, the bleeding chest,
tamponade at the bedside, refractory vasoplegia, sudden VF on the table, massive
air embolism, and the 2 a.m. type A dissection (the long-form flagship sim).

    filterable deck (All / Curriculum / Anatomy / Surgery / by day / by topic /
    by operation) with a single unified due-count in the nav badge.
  - Keep the flip + Again/Hard/Good/Easy + keys 1–4 interaction (it works).
  - Curriculum/surgery cards stay lesson/article-gated; anatomy cards stay
    always-available. Preserve progress on migration.
  - Add a "leech" surfacing (cards repeatedly failed) and a weakness-weighted new-
    card selection so the SRS actually targets the user's gaps.

F.3  MASTERY MODEL (per-topic, not just global XP)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - Replace the single XP number with a per-domain mastery map (Anatomy, Coronary,
    Valves, Aorta, CPB, MCS, ICU, Hemostasis, Rhythm, plus per-operation mastery
    for the Surgeries tab). Mastery rises from lesson completion, SRS retention,
    MCQ accuracy at judgment level, walkthrough smoothness, and simulation scores.
  - Surface it as a radar/heat map on the Progress page so the student sees, at a
    glance, "you are PGY5 on valves, PGY2 on the aorta — drill the aorta."
  - Keep XP/streaks/badges as the engagement layer on top.

F.4  THE PGY5 CERTIFICATION (replaces the single mock exam)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Day 14 becomes a multi-stage capstone that actually tests the A.3 bar:
  - Stage 1: a broad written exam (the existing 75-MCQ format, but drawn to hit
    every domain and weighted to judgment-level items).
  - Stage 2: two "long cases" — a patient dossier the user works end to end
    (indication → operation choice → operative plan → predicted complications →
    first-24-h plan), scored against a rubric derived from A.3(a)–(f).
  - Stage 3: a rapid-fire viva — 15–20 "what's the next step / why / what's the
    exception" items on a clock, oral-board style.
  - Stage 4: one crisis simulation run cold (from D.3), scored on recognition and
    management.
  - A pass on all four unlocks a "PGY5-Ready" credential on the dashboard. This
    gives the whole app a summit to climb toward and makes "PGY5 level" a thing
    the app can actually award, not just claim.

================================================================================
PART G — EXPONENTIAL IMPROVEMENTS (raise the ceiling, not just the floor)
================================================================================

These are the ideas that change what the app IS, ordered by leverage.

G.1  A "CASE OF THE DAY" ENGINE
    A daily generated (from a large authored case bank) integrated case that
    forces synthesis across domains — the single best habit-former and the thing
    that most resembles real training. Feeds mastery and streaks.

G.2  ADAPTIVE PATHING
    Use the mastery map (F.3) to reorder what the app surfaces: weak domains get
    more new cards, harder MCQs, and targeted walkthroughs; strong domains get
    maintenance-only SRS. The 14-day spine becomes the default, not the ceiling.

G.3  A SPACED-REPETITION LAYER OVER IMAGING AND OPERATIVE STEPS
    Not just facts — put "name this view," "what's the next operative step," and
    "grade this lesion" into the SRS so recognition and sequence become durable.

G.4  "TEACH-BACK" / FREE-RECALL PROMPTS
    Periodically, instead of an MCQ, ask the user to type a free-text answer
    ("explain why LIMA-to-LAD," "narrate weaning from bypass") and then reveal a
    model answer for self-grading. Free recall beats recognition for retention and
    directly rehearses the viva. (Self-graded to stay offline and dependency-free;
    an optional LLM-graded mode is a natural later add if connectivity is allowed.)

G.5  A GLOSSARY / QUICK-REFERENCE LAYER
    A hover/tap glossary so any term (EROA, TAPSE, del Nido, INTERMACS) expands in
    place, plus a searchable "numbers" reference (every threshold and target in the
    app on one filterable page). This is what residents actually want at 3 a.m.

G.6  AUDIO MODE
    Short authored audio narrations of the surgery articles and Attending Rounds
    for commute/pre-round listening. Even text-to-speech of the existing prose is a
    meaningful accessibility and engagement win.

G.7  A "PIMP QUESTIONS" DECK PER TOPIC
    The classic OR pimping questions ("what's the blood supply to the SA node?"
    "why does the mammary stay open?") as a rapid-fire mode — the highest-morale,
    most-shared feature a surgical study app can have.

G.8  SHAREABLE PROGRESS & LIGHT SOCIAL
    Exportable mastery/credential cards and an optional local leaderboard for a
    clerkship group. Purely opt-in; keep the app offline-first and privacy-first.

G.9  INSTRUCTOR/CLERKSHIP MODE
    A way for a clerkship director to define the day-1 date, required modules, and
    a target completion, and to hand the app to a rotation of students. Turns a
    personal tool into a program-adoptable curriculum.

G.10 CONTENT PROVENANCE & UPDATING
    Every threshold/trial statement carries a lightweight source tag (guideline
    year, trial name) so the content is auditable and can be updated as guidelines
    change. Cardiac surgery evidence moves; the app should be maintainable, not
    frozen.

================================================================================
PART H — CONTENT AUTHORING STANDARDS (one voice, PGY5 register)
================================================================================

So that thousands of new words read as one authored resource:

  - VOICE: precise, confident, senior-resident register. Explain mechanism first,
    then evidence, then exception. Never hand-wave a "why."
  - EVERY claim that carries a number states the number, and where a threshold
    comes from a guideline or trial, name it.
  - LEAD WITH PROSE: each teaching chunk opens with 1–2 explanatory paragraphs
    before any list (the existing lessons already do this — hold the line).
  - KBOXES used deliberately: mnemonic / pearl / danger / study, each with the
    opening tag div, never as decoration.
  - ASSUME ZERO PRIOR KNOWLEDGE at the top of every surgery article and EVERY
    Attending Rounds module, then climb to PGY5 by the end. The novice must be
    able to start cold; the senior must find something new by the end.
  - AUTHORING HYGIENE (matches the existing build rules): real, balanced HTML;
    no backtick or ${ inside content strings; globally unique ids in the
    established formats (fc-*, q-*, lesson ids, surgery ids); 0-based answer index
    with a teaching explanation that addresses the distractors.
  - EVERY new content unit must pass the relevant validator at 0 errors before it
    is considered done, and must node --check clean. New modules ship their own
    validator, mirroring the anatomy/trials isolation pattern.
  - GROUNDING: build from the existing source set (cardiac_surgery_guide_v3.pdf,
    the primer, the anatomy deck) plus current guideline/trial knowledge; do not
    invent statistics — every stat is traceable.
